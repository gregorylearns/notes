# F.1 - Metabolic
## Summary


### METABOLIC DISORDERS: NEUROLOGIC COMPLICATIONS

| Topic | Key Features and Pathogenesis | Clinical Manifestations | Diagnosis and Management |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <span style="color:red">Alcohol (Ethanol) Intoxication</span> | **Acute intoxication** relates to GABA receptors and chloride-ion channels; functions as a CNS depressant. | **Functional impairment** varies by blood alcohol level: 100mg/dL (Ataxia), 200mg/dL (Confusion), 300mg/dL (Stupor), 400mg/dL (Deep anesthesia/Fatal). | **Management** of non-ethanol (Methyl/Ethylene glycol) intoxication involves large amounts of IV bicarbonate and hemodialysis. |
| <span style="color:red">Alcohol Withdrawal Syndrome</span> | **Pathophysiology** involves CNS hyperexcitability due to decreased GABA (inhibitory) and increased Glutamate (excitatory) activity. | **Early signs** (6-24 hrs) include tremors and "the jitters"; **Withdrawal seizures** (7-48 hrs) are generalized tonic-clonic; **DTs** (2-4 days) involve autonomic hyperactivity. | **First-line treatment** is Benzodiazepines (Diazepam, Chlordiazepoxide); **CIWA-Ar Scale** is used to assess severity and guide treatment. |
| <span style="color:red">Delirium Tremens (DTs)</span> | **Life-threatening form** of alcohol withdrawal occurring 2-4 days after cessation; characterized by delirium and autonomic hyperactivity. | **Clinical features** include vivid visual hallucinations, delusions, fever, tachycardia, diaphoresis, and severe insomnia. | **Management** includes frequent vital signs monitoring, fluids (up to 5L/day), and IV Diazepam/Lorazepam; mortality is ~5%. |
| <span style="color:red">Wernicke Encephalopathy</span> | **Acute emergency** caused by severe Vitamin B1 (thiamine) deficiency; often precipitated by glucose administration without thiamine. | **Classic Triad**: Confusion, Ophthalmoplegia (nystagmus), and Ataxia. | **Emergency Management**: Administer 50–500 mg parenteral thiamine BEFORE or alongside glucose; add Magnesium as it is a required cofactor. |
| <span style="color:red">Korsakoff Syndrome</span> | **Chronic neuropsychiatric sequela** of Wernicke encephalopathy; involves permanent damage to mammillary bodies and medial thalami. | **Main signs**: Severe anterograde amnesia (inability to form new memories) and confabulation. | **Prognosis**: While ocular signs improve, the amnesic syndrome recovers entirely in fewer than 20% of patients. |
| <span style="color:red">Alcoholic Cerebellar Degeneration</span> | **Chronic degeneration** of the cerebellar vermis (Purkinje cells) due to thiamine deficiency and direct alcohol toxicity. | **Hallmark**: Wide-based, unsteady gait and truncal instability; lower limbs are much more affected than upper limbs. | **Diagnosis** shows atrophy of the anterior/superior vermis on MRI; management focuses on total abstinence and thiamine. |
| <span style="color:red">Marchiafava-Bignami Disease</span> | **Rare necrosis** of the corpus callosum; historically linked to heavy red wine consumption and malnutrition. | **Common signs**: Sudden altered mental status, seizures, and "callosal disconnection syndrome" (left-sided apraxia). | **Diagnosis** is made via MRI showing the "sandwich sign" (sparing of dorsal/ventral callosal edges); managed with high-dose B-complex. |
| <span style="color:red">Fetal Alcohol Syndrome (FAS)</span> | **Teratogenic effect** of in-utero alcohol exposure sparking neuronal apoptosis and growth restriction. | **Classic Triad**: Growth restriction, facial abnormalities (thin upper lip, smooth philtrum), and CNS dysfunction (ADHD-like). | **Epidemiology**: Affects ~1/3 of infants born to heavy drinkers; most critical window is the first trimester (organogenesis). |
| <span style="color:red">Vitamin B1 (Thiamine) Deficiency</span> | **Energy failure** due to dysfunction of pyruvate dehydrogenase and transketolase; affects high-demand tissues. | **Dry Beriberi**: Symmetrical distal sensorimotor polyneuropathy. **Wet Beriberi**: Neuropathy plus high-output heart failure. | **Management**: Prompt thiamine replacement; deficits in Dry Beriberi may be only partially reversible. |
| <span style="color:red">Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin) Deficiency</span> | **Impaired mitochondrial metabolism** due to loss of FAD and FMN cofactors in the electron transport chain. | **Diagnostic clues**: Angular cheilitis, glossitis, seborrheic dermatitis, and night blindness. | **Genetic defects** (e.g., Brown-Vialetto-Van Laere) can cause motor neuron disease-like symptoms and sensorineural hearing loss. |
| <span style="color:red">Vitamin B3 (Niacin) Deficiency</span> | **Pellagra** results from lack of niacin or tryptophan (precursor), common in corn-heavy diets. | **Classic "3 Ds"**: Dermatitis, Diarrhea, and Dementia; also involves "cogwheel rigidity" in acute encephalopathy. | **Management**: Nicotinamide is preferred over nicotinic acid to avoid the "flushing" side effect. |
| <span style="color:red">Vitamin B6 (Pyridoxine) Deficiency</span> | **Disrupted neurotransmitter synthesis** (GABA, Serotonin, Dopamine) and heme synthesis. | **Unique findings**: Sideroblastic anemia, peripheral neuropathy, and seizures (due to decreased GABA). | **Clinical Pearl**: Isoniazid (INH) and Hydralazine are common drug-induced causes; supplemental B6 is required. |
| <span style="color:red">Vitamin B9 (Folate) Deficiency</span> | **Impaired DNA synthesis** and methylation; absorbed in the jejunum under acidic conditions. | **Clinical features**: Macrocytic anemia and increased risk of neural tube defects in pregnancy. | **Drug Interference**: Long-term Phenytoin or Methotrexate use are common causes of folate-deficient polyneuropathy. |
| <span style="color:red">Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin) Deficiency</span> | **Impaired myelin maintenance** due to accumulation of methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine. | **Hallmark**: Subacute Combined Degeneration (SCD) affecting posterior (proprioception) and lateral (corticospinal) columns. | **Warning**: Giving Folic acid alone corrects anemia but allows neurologic damage (SCD) to progress or worsen. |
| <span style="color:red">Diabetic Distal Polyneuropathy</span> | **Most common** diabetic neuropathy; length-dependent microvascular damage leads to fiber loss. | **Pattern**: Chronic, symmetric "stocking-glove" sensory loss; absent ankle jerks; risk of Charcot joints. | **Pharmacology**: First-line for pain are Duloxetine, Pregabalin, Gabapentin, or Amitriptyline. |
| <span style="color:red">Diabetic Mononeuropathies</span> | **Ischemic infarction** of single nerves, more common in older patients with long-standing diabetes. | **Classic Presentation**: Painful third (III) nerve palsy with pupillary sparing; also affects femoral and peroneal nerves. | **Prognosis**: Recovery is common but typically takes several months. |
| <span style="color:red">Diabetic Amyotrophy</span> | **Radiculoplexus neuropathy** characterized by severe proximal pain and muscle wasting. | **Manifestation**: Severe back/hip pain followed by weakness and atrophy of the pelvic girdle and thigh; loss of patellar reflex. | **CSF findings**: Protein may be significantly elevated (50–150 mg/dL). |
| <span style="color:red">Leprosy (Hansen Disease) Neuropathy</span> | **Infectious neuropathy** caused by M. leprae invading Schwann cells via PGL-1 binding. | **Hallmark Pattern**: Mononeuritis multiplex (asymmetric involvement of multiple non-contiguous nerves). | **Management**: 3-drug regimen (Rifampicin, Clofazimine, Dapsone); 6 months for Paucibacillary, 12 months for Multibacillary. |
| <span style="color:red">Uremic Encephalopathy (UE)</span> | **Reversible functional disorder** due to accumulation of metabolic toxins (urea, organic acids, PTH). | **Hallmarks**: Fluctuating consciousness, asterixis (flapping tremor), and "uremic twitch-convulsive syndrome" (asynchronous myoclonus). | **Definitive Treatment**: Urgent dialysis ("U" in AEIOU); symptoms typically improve within 1-2 days post-dialysis. |
| <span style="color:red">Hyperglycemic Encephalopathy</span> | **Metabolic crisis** split into Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) and Hyperosmolar Nonketotic Hyperglycemia (HHS). | **HHS**: Glucose >600, severe dehydration, focal neuro deficits (mimics stroke), and seizures common. | **Treatment Warning**: Rapid correction of glucose in children with DKA can trigger cerebral edema. |

### SUPPLEMENTAL FLASHCARD FACTS

*   **Alcohol Levels**: In <span style="color:red">Alcohol-Related Neurologic Complications</span>, a blood alcohol level of 100mg/dL typically causes ataxia, while 400mg/dL causes deep anesthesia and is potentially fatal.
*   **Withdrawal Timeline**: For <span style="color:red">Alcohol Withdrawal Syndrome</span>, tremors occur within 6-24 hours, seizures peak at 13-24 hours, and Delirium Tremens (DTs) typically start 48-72 hours after the last drink.
*   **Wernicke Triad**: In <span style="color:red">Vitamin B1 (Thiamine) Deficiency</span>, the diagnostic triad consists of confusion, ophthalmoplegia (nystagmus), and ataxia, but all three are present in less than 1/3 of patients. 
*   **Beriberi Differentiator**: In <span style="color:red">Thiamine Deficiency</span>, "Dry Beriberi" refers to peripheral neuropathy, while "Wet Beriberi" adds high-output heart failure (edema).
*   **Pellagra Triad**: In <span style="color:red">Vitamin B3 (Niacin) Deficiency</span>, the hallmark is the "3 Ds": Dermatitis, Diarrhea, and Dementia.
*   **Isoniazid Interaction**: In <span style="color:red">Vitamin B6 (Pyridoxine) Deficiency</span>, clinicians must give 50mg/day of B6 to patients taking Isoniazid (INH) to prevent drug-induced neuropathy. 
*   **Nitrous Oxide Risk**: In <span style="color:red">Vitamin B12 Deficiency</span>, exposure to Nitrous Oxide (N2O) can acutely inactivate B12-dependent enzymes and precipitate Subacute Combined Degeneration.
*   **SCD Columns**: In <span style="color:red">Vitamin B12 Deficiency</span>, Subacute Combined Degeneration involves the posterior columns (loss of vibration/proprioception) and lateral corticospinal tracts (spasticity/weakness).
*   **Pupillary Sparing**: In <span style="color:red">Diabetic Mononeuropathy</span>, a painful third nerve palsy typically shows "pupillary sparing," which helps differentiate it from a compressive aneurysm.
*   **HHS Seizures**: In <span style="color:red">Hyperglycemic Encephalopathy</span>, seizures and focal deficits (like hemiparesis) are much more common in HHS than in DKA.
*   **Leprosy Pattern**: For <span style="color:red">Leprosy (Hansen Disease)</span>, the most characteristic neurologic pattern is mononeuritis multiplex, involving thickened, palpable nerves (Ulnar, Common Peroneal). 
*   **Lepra Sensory Loss**: In <span style="color:red">Leprosy Neuropathy</span>, the sequence of sensory loss is Temperature -> Touch -> Pain.
*   **Myoclonus Pattern**: In <span style="color:red">Uremic Encephalopathy</span>, uremic twitching or myoclonus is notably asynchronous, meaning the two sides of the body do not jerk in the same pattern.
*   **Lentiform Fork Sign**: In <span style="color:red">Uremic Encephalopathy</span>, a rare MRI finding showing bilateral hyperintensities in the basal ganglia is called the "Lentiform Fork Sign."
*   **Dialysis Disequilibrium**: In <span style="color:red">Uremic Encephalopathy Management</span>, rapid removal of urea during dialysis can cause brain edema, known as Dialysis Disequilibrium Syndrome (DDS).
*   **DDS Prevention**: To prevent DDS in <span style="color:red">Uremic Encephalopathy</span>, initial dialysis sessions should be "low and slow" (2 hours, low flow rates).
*   **Phenytoin Danger**: In <span style="color:red">Uremic Encephalopathy</span>, Phenytoin is contraindicated for seizures because uremia alters its protein binding, leading to severe neurotoxicity despite normal serum levels.
*   **Uremic Fetor**: In <span style="color:red">Uremic Encephalopathy</span>, the patient may have "uremic fetor," which is a distinctive urine-like breath odor.

### CRITICAL DIFFERENTIATIONS FOR EXAMS

*   <span style="color:red">Alcoholic Hallucinosis vs. Delirium Tremens</span>: In Hallucinosis, the patient has a clear sensorium (oriented, intact memory), whereas in DTs, the patient is profoundly confused and disoriented.
*   <span style="color:red">Wernicke vs. Korsakoff</span>: Wernicke is the acute phase (confusion, eye signs, ataxia) and is reversible; Korsakoff is the chronic phase (anterograde amnesia, confabulation) and is often permanent.
*   <span style="color:red">B12 vs. Folate Deficiency Labs</span>: Both show macrocytic anemia and high homocysteine, but ONLY Vitamin B12 deficiency shows elevated Methylmalonic Acid (MMA).
*   <span style="color:red">Tuberculoid vs. Lepromatous Leprosy</span>: Tuberculoid leprosy (strong immunity) has few bacilli (paucibacillary) and localized patches; Lepromatous leprosy (poor immunity) has numerous bacilli (multibacillary) and diffuse symmetric loss.
*   <span style="color:red">Withdrawal Seizures vs. Alcohol-Triggered Epilepsy</span>: Withdrawal seizures occur 7-48 hours after stopping alcohol; alcohol-triggered epilepsy occurs during the "sobering up" or hangover period in patients with baseline epilepsy.
*   <span style="color:red">DKA vs. HHS</span>: DKA presents with metabolic acidosis, Kussmaul breathing, and ketones; HHS presents with massive hyperglycemia (>600), high osmolality (>330), and frequent focal neurologic signs.
*   <span style="color:red">Uremic Encephalopathy vs. Hepatic Encephalopathy</span>: Both feature asterixis, but uremic encephalopathy is unique for its "twitch-convulsive syndrome" (asynchronous myoclonus) and uremic fetor.
*   <span style="color:red">Paucibacillary vs. Multibacillary Leprosy Treatment</span>: Paucibacillary requires 6 months of Multi-Drug Therapy (MDT); Multibacillary requires 12 months.
*   <span style="color:red">Dialysis Dementia vs. Dialysis Disequilibrium Syndrome</span>: DDS is an acute osmotic event during/after dialysis; Dialysis Dementia is a chronic, progressive decline formerly linked to aluminum toxicity.
*   <span style="color:red">Vitamin B6 Deficiency vs. Toxicity</span>: B6 deficiency causes peripheral neuropathy and seizures; B6 toxicity (excessive intake) causes a pure sensory ganglionopathy with ataxia but no weakness.

## Developmental
## Summary


| GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF DEVELOPMENTAL DISORDERS |
| :--- |
| **Pathogenesis** | The <font color="red">nervous system</font> requires the longest time for development and maturation, making it uniquely susceptible to disease over an extended period. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | A <font color="red">nervous system maldevelopment</font> is characterized by being present at birth and remaining stable and nonprogressive thereafter. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | In infants with major malformations, the <font color="red">nervous system</font> is involved in the majority of cases. |
| **Diagnosis** | If one or two malformations are observed in an infant, clinicians must investigate for <font color="red">nervous system involvement</font> due to the high frequency of association. |
| **Important Note** | Changes in the clinical manifestations of <font color="red">developmental disorders</font> as the brain matures do not necessarily mean the underlying disease is progressive. |

| HYDRANENCEPHALY |
| :--- |
| **Pathogenesis** | <font color="red">Hydranencephaly</font> is caused by bilateral internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusion during early fetal development, leading to absent cerebral hemispheres. |
| **Pathogenesis** | In <font color="red">hydranencephaly</font>, the brainstem and the basal portion of the forebrain typically remain intact despite the absence of cerebral hemispheres. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | Patients with <font color="red">hydranencephaly</font> are typically normocephalic at birth, but develop an excessive increase in head circumference (HC) postnatally due to CSF accumulation. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | Characteristics of <font color="red">hydranencephaly</font> include irritability, poor feeding, seizures, spastic quadriparesis, and little to no cognitive development. |
| **Diagnosis** | A hallmark finding for <font color="red">hydranencephaly</font> is a positive transillumination test, where the skull glows when a light is flashed against it in a dark room. |
| **Treatment** | The primary treatment for <font color="red">hydranencephaly</font> is a VP shunt, which is often cosmetic and intended to make patient care easier. |

| CRANIOSYNOSTOSIS |
| :--- |
| **Pathogenesis** | <font color="red">Craniosynostosis</font> is defined as the premature closure of one or more cranial sutures. |
| **Pathogenesis** | <font color="red">Primary craniosynostosis</font> is due to abnormalities of skull development, while <font color="red">secondary craniosynostosis</font> is due to failure of brain growth and expansion. |
| **Pathogenesis** | In <font color="red">craniosynostosis</font>, skull growth is restricted perpendicular to the closed suture, leading to compensatory growth in other directions and increased intracranial pressure (IICP). |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | Physical findings of <font color="red">craniosynostosis</font> include a progressive skull deformity and a palpable prominent bony ridge over the fused suture. |
| **Diagnosis** | <font color="red">Positional plagiocephaly</font> is a key differential for craniosynostosis; it is acquired from external pressure, has no suture fusion, and does not require surgery. |
| **Unique Finding** | In <font color="red">craniosynostosis</font>, the anterior fontanelle is diamond-shaped, while the posterior fontanelle is triangle-shaped. |
| **Treatment** | Surgical management for <font color="red">craniosynostosis</font> involves vault expansion or midface advancement, often requiring significant blood products due to bone cutting. |

| TYPES OF CRANIOSYNOSTOSIS |
| :--- |
| **Sagittal Synostosis** | <font color="red">Sagittal synostosis</font> is the most common type (80% male), resulting in **scaphocephaly** (boat-shaped head) with a long AP diameter and biparietal narrowing. |
| **Metopic Synostosis** | <font color="red">Metopic synostosis</font> is the second most common type, resulting in **trigonocephaly** (triangle-shaped head) with a narrow, keel-shaped forehead and hypotelorism. |
| **Unicoronal Synostosis** | <font color="red">Unicoronal synostosis</font> results in **frontal plagiocephaly** (trapezium-shaped), showing a flattened forehead and a wider-appearing eye on the ipsilateral side. |
| **Unilambdoid Synostosis** | <font color="red">Unilambdoid synostosis</font> results in **posterior plagiocephaly** (trapezium-shaped) with mastoid bulging and a "wind-swept" appearance of the skull. |
| **Positional Plagiocephaly** | <font color="red">Positional plagiocephaly</font> presents as a **parallelogram-shaped head** with flattening on one side of the back and fullness on the same side of the front, without mastoid bulging. |

| GENETIC SYNDROMES ASSOCIATED WITH CRANIOSYNOSTOSIS |
| :--- |
| **Crouzon Syndrome** | <font color="red">Crouzon syndrome</font> (AD) is characterized by brachycephaly, pansynostosis, proptosis (due to underdeveloped orbits), maxillary hypoplasia, and acanthosis nigricans. |
| **Apert Syndrome** | <font color="red">Apert syndrome</font> (Sporadic > AD) presents with Crouzon-like features plus symmetric syndactyly (mitten hands), acne, and developmental delay. |
| **Saethre-Chotzen** | <font color="red">Saethre-Chotzen syndrome</font> (AD) involves coronal synostosis, ptosis, and syndactyly specifically of the 2nd and 3rd digits. |
| **Muenke Syndrome** | <font color="red">Muenke syndrome</font> is the most prevalent syndrome, presenting with bi-coronal synostosis, sensorineural hearing loss, and behavioral issues. |

| HOLOPROSENCEPHALY (HPE) |
| :--- |
| **Pathogenesis** | <font color="red">Holoprosencephaly</font> results from a failure of the embryonic prosencephalon (forebrain) to undergo cleavage into two distinct cerebral hemispheres. |
| **Etiology** | The most common chromosomal cause of <font color="red">holoprosencephaly</font> is Trisomy 13 (60%), while maternal diabetes is a significant environmental risk factor. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | The spectrum of facial abnormalities in <font color="red">holoprosencephaly</font> includes cyclopia (single eye), cebocephaly (single nostril), and a solitary central incisor. |
| **Classification** | <font color="red">Alobar holoprosencephaly</font> is the most severe form, featuring complete fusion of cerebral hemispheres/deep nuclei and a midline monoventricle. |
| **Classification** | <font color="red">Semilobar holoprosencephaly</font> involves fusion of the anterior cerebral hemispheres and absence of the anterior corpus callosum. |
| **Classification** | <font color="red">Lobar holoprosencephaly</font> is the least severe form, with nearly full separation of hemispheres and only subtle facial dysmorphism. |
| **Classification** | <font color="red">Syntelencephaly</font> is a variant characterized by non-separation specifically of the posterior frontal and parietal lobes, appearing like the letter "H". |
| **Prognosis** | High mortality within the first year of life is expected for the <font color="red">alobar and syndromic forms of holoprosencephaly</font>. |

| LISSENCEPHALY AND SCHIZENCEPHALY |
| :--- |
| **Pathogenesis** | <font color="red">Lissencephaly</font> is caused by faulty neuronal migration, resulting in a "smooth brain" appearance devoid of cerebral convolutions (sulci and gyri). |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Lissencephaly</font> presents with failure to thrive (FTT), progressive microcephaly, severe developmental delay, and refractory epilepsy. |
| **Unique Finding** | <font color="red">Pachygyria</font> is a milder form of the lissencephaly spectrum, characterized by a markedly reduced number of abnormally thick gyri. |
| **Miller-Dieker Syndrome** | <font color="red">Miller-Dieker syndrome</font> combines lissencephaly with specific facies: prominent forehead, bitemporal hollowing, anteverted nostrils, and micrognathia. |
| **Walker-Warburg** | <font color="red">Walker-Warburg syndrome</font> is associated with "cobblestone" lissencephaly due to neuronal overmigration past the cortex. |
| **Schizencephaly** | <font color="red">Schizencephaly</font> is characterized by unilateral or bilateral clefts in the cerebral hemispheres that may be "open lip" (communicating with ventricles) or "closed lip." |
| **Etiology** | <font color="red">Schizencephaly</font> has been associated with fetal CMV infection or specific genetic mutations. |

| NEUROFIBROMATOSIS TYPE 1 (NF1) |
| :--- |
| **Pathogenesis** | <font color="red">Neurofibromatosis 1</font> is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a loss-of-function mutation in the *NF1* gene, which encodes neurofibromin (a RAS pathway inhibitor). |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Café-au-lait macules</font> are the hallmark of NF1, appearing at birth and increasing in size and number; at least 6 are required (size >5mm prepubertal, >15mm postpubertal). |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Axillary or inguinal freckling</font>, also known as "sun-shy freckling," typically appears between ages 3 to 5 years in children with NF1. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Iris Lisch nodules</font> are hamartomas of the iris found in nearly 100% of NF1 patients over age 21. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Plexiform neurofibromas</font> in NF1 involve diffuse thickening of nerve trunks and can lead to overgrowth of extremities and bone deformity. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Optic pathway glioma</font> is the most common CNS tumor in NF1, typically manifesting between ages 2 and 6 years. |
| **Diagnosis** | The diagnosis of <font color="red">NF1</font> is clinical; genetic testing is NOT required if clinical criteria (2 out of 7 manifestations) are met. |
| **Complications** | <font color="red">Moyamoya</font> syndrome, characterized by progressive narrowing of the internal carotid arteries, can occur in patients with NF1 and lead to stroke. |
| **Management** | <font color="red">Selumetinib</font> is an FDA-approved MEK inhibitor used for children >2 years old with inoperable, symptomatic plexiform neurofibromas in NF1. |

| TUBEROUS SCLEROSIS COMPLEX (TSC) |
| :--- |
| **Pathogenesis** | <font color="red">Tuberous sclerosis</font> is an autosomal dominant disorder involving mutations in *TSC1* (hamartin) or *TSC2* (tuberin) that lead to overactive mTOR signaling. |
| **Pathogenesis** | <font color="red">TSC2 mutations</font> are generally associated with a more severe clinical phenotype compared to TSC1 mutations. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Cortical tubers</font> are the hallmark CNS lesion in TSC and are a major cause of epilepsy and developmental delay. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Subependymal nodules (SENs)</font> occur along the lateral ventricular walls and are described as looking like "candle dripping" on imaging. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Subependymal Giant Cell Astrocytoma (SEGA)</font> in TSC can grow and cause obstructive hydrocephalus, requiring surgery or mTOR inhibitors. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | The earliest skin lesions in TSC are <font color="red">hypomelanotic macules</font> (ash-leaf spots); at least 3 are required for major diagnostic criteria. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Facial angiofibromas</font> are red, acne-like nodules on the nose and cheeks that typically appear between ages 4 to 6 years in TSC patients. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Shagreen patches</font> in TSC are rough, raised areas of skin with an "orange-peel" consistency, usually found on the lower back. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Cardiac rhabdomyomas</font> are benign heart tumors in TSC often detected prenatally that tend to resolve spontaneously postnatally. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Renal angiomyolipomas</font> in TSC are symptomatic in adulthood (pain, hematuria) and should be managed with Everolimus to avoid nephrectomy. |
| **Clinical Manifestations** | <font color="red">Pulmonary lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM)</font> is a TSC-associated lung disease that almost exclusively affects females. |
| **Diagnosis** | A diagnosis of <font color="red">TSC</font> requires 2 major criteria, OR 1 major and 2 minor criteria, OR the identification of a TSC1/TSC2 variant. |
| **Treatment** | <font color="red">Vigabatrin</font> is the first-line treatment for infantile spasms associated with Tuberous Sclerosis Complex. |
| **Treatment** | <font color="red">Everolimus or Sirolimus</font> (mTOR inhibitors) are used to treat SEGAs, renal angiomyolipomas, and drug-resistant epilepsy in TSC. |

| CEREBRAL PALSY (CP) |
| :--- |
| **Definition** | <font color="red">Cerebral palsy</font> is a group of permanent, nonprogressive central motor dysfunctions caused by an early insult to the developing fetal or infant brain. |
| **Etiology** | The most common cause of acquired CP is <font color="red">perinatal stroke</font>, followed by meningitis and encephalitis. |
| **Risk Factors** | <font color="red">Preterm infants</font> are at highest risk for CP due to intracerebral hemorrhage and periventricular leukomalacia (PVL). |
| **Pathogenesis** | <font color="red">Periventricular leukomalacia (PVL)</font> reflects the vulnerability of immature oligodendroglia to hypoxia and oxidative stress in preterm infants. |
| **Classification** | <font color="red">Spastic CP</font> is the most common form (80%), involving the upper motor neuron (UMN) pathways. |
| **Classification** | <font color="red">Spastic diplegia</font> is strongly associated with prematurity; it affects the legs more than the arms and is characterized by a "commando crawl." |
| **Classification** | <font color="red">Spastic hemiplegia</font> presents with UMN signs on one side of the body, arm involvement greater than the leg, and early handedness (before 12 months). |
| **Classification** | <font color="red">Spastic quadriplegia</font> is the most severe form of CP, with all limbs affected and a high frequency of ID, seizures, and feeding difficulties. |
| **Classification** | <font color="red">Dyskinetic (Athetoid) CP</font> is often caused by kernicterus or HIE and is characterized by slow, writhing movements and a persistent asymmetric tonic neck reflex. |
| **Diagnosis** | The diagnosis of <font color="red">cerebral palsy</font> requires a history of motor delay; if a child regression or loses previously attained milestones, the diagnosis is NOT CP. |
| **Management** | Management of <font color="red">cerebral palsy</font> is multidisciplinary, using physical therapy, orthotics, and medications like Baclofen or Botulinum toxin (BoNT-A) for spasticity. |

| DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES AND COMPARISONS |
| :--- |
| 1. <font color="red">Hydranencephaly</font> shows a positive transillumination test, whereas <font color="red">hydrocephalus</font> preserves a rim of cerebral cortex. |
| 2. <font color="red">Sagittal synostosis</font> causes a boat-shaped head (scaphocephaly), while <font color="red">metopic synostosis</font> causes a triangle-shaped forehead (trigonocephaly). |
| 3. <font color="red">Positional plagiocephaly</font> features a parallelogram-shaped head and anteriorly displaced ear, unlike <font color="red">unilambdoid synostosis</font> which has a posteriorly displaced ear and mastoid bulging. |
| 4. <font color="red">Primary craniosynostosis</font> is a defect of the skull bones, whereas <font color="red">secondary craniosynostosis</font> results from the brain's failure to grow. |
| 5. <font color="red">Apert syndrome</font> features complex symmetric syndactyly ("mitten hands"), which distinguishes it from the simpler syndactyly of <font color="red">Saethre-Chotzen</font> 2nd/3rd digits. |
| 6. <font color="red">Alobar holoprosencephaly</font> is characterized by a single midline monoventricle, while <font color="red">Lobar HPE</font> has nearly full hemispheric separation. |
| 7. <font color="red">Lissencephaly</font> involves a completely smooth brain surface, whereas <font color="red">pachygyria</font> still has a few, albeit very thick, gyri. |
| 8. <font color="red">NF1</font> skin lesions include Café-au-lait macules and axillary freckling, whereas <font color="red">TSC</font> skin lesions include ash-leaf spots and facial angiofibromas. |
| 9. <font color="red">Iris Lisch nodules</font> (NF1) are surface hamartomas of the iris, while <font color="red">retinal hamartomas</font> (TSC) are mulberry-like lesions on the retina. |
| 10. <font color="red">Neurofibromatosis 1</font> diagnosis is primarily based on clinical criteria, while <font color="red">Tuberous Sclerosis</font> diagnosis can be made by clinical criteria OR identifying a pathogenic variant. |
| 11. <font color="red">Optic pathway glioma</font> is the classic tumor associated with NF1, whereas <font color="red">SEGA</font> is the classic brain tumor associated with TSC. |
| 12. <font color="red">Selumetinib</font> is targeted for plexiform neurofibromas in NF1, while <font color="red">Everolimus</font> is targeted for SEGAs and renal AMLs in TSC. |
| 13. <font color="red">Spastic diplegia</font> in CP affects the lower extremities more than upper, whereas <font color="red">spastic hemiplegia</font> affects one side of the body with the arm usually worse than the leg. |
| 14. <font color="red">Spastic CP</font> results from UMN pathway damage, while <font color="red">Extrapyramidal (Dyskinetic) CP</font> results from damage to basal ganglia or cerebellum. |
| 15. <font color="red">Athetoid CP</font> shows slow twisting movements and is linked to Kernicterus, whereas <font color="red">Ataxic CP</font> is characterized by wide-based gait and poor coordination. |
| 16. <font color="red">Commando crawling</font> (dragging legs) is a sign of spastic diplegia, whereas <font color="red">early handedness</font> (using one hand before age 1) is a sign of spastic hemiplegia. |
| 17. <font color="red">Crouzon Syndrome</font> typically includes proptosis and maxillary hypoplasia without the syndactyly seen in <font color="red">Apert Syndrome</font>. |
| 18. <font color="red">TSC1</font> (hamartin) mutations are generally less severe clinically than <font color="red">TSC2</font> (tuberin) mutations. |
| 19. <font color="red">Muenke syndrome</font> is uniquely associated with sensorineural hearing loss among the common craniosynostosis syndromes. |
| 20. <font color="red">Schizencephaly</font> involves a cleft through the brain mantle, while <font color="red">Porencephaly</font> (a differential) is typically a smooth-walled cyst within the brain parenchyma. |
| 21. <font color="red">Holoprosencephaly</font> is a defect of cleavage/division, while <font color="red">Lissencephaly</font> is a defect of neuronal migration. |
| 22. <font color="red">Cardiac rhabdomyomas</font> in TSC tend to regress and disappear over time, whereas <font color="red">Renal angiomyolipomas</font> tend to grow and become symptomatic in adulthood. |
| 23. <font color="red">Cerebral Palsy</font> is permanent and nonprogressive, whereas a <font color="red">neurodegenerative disorder</font> would show a decline or loss of milestones (regression). |
| 24. <font color="red">Vigabatrin</font> is specific for infantile spasms in TSC, but <font color="red">Everolimus</font> is used for generalized drug-resistant focal epilepsy in TSC. |
| 25. <font color="red">Plexiform neurofibroma</font> (NF1) feels like a "bag of worms," whereas <font color="red">facial angiofibromas</font> (TSC) are firm, red, acne-like papules. |
