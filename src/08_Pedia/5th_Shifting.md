### GENERAL PRINCIPLES AND EVALUATION BASICS

*   In <span style="color:red">Anemia</span>, the condition is defined as a reduction of hemoglobin concentration or RBC volume below the range for healthy persons of the same age and sex.
*   <span style="color:red">Physiologic Adjustments to Anemia</span> include increased cardiac output, augmented oxygen extraction, shunting of blood to vital organs, and increased concentration of 2,3-DPG.
*   The <span style="color:red">Oxygen-Dissociation Curve</span> in anemia demonstrates a "Shift to the Right," which reduces hemoglobin's affinity for O2 to facilitate better tissue transfer.
*   A <span style="color:red">Limited Hematologic Evaluation</span> for anemic children consists of a CBC with red cell indices, platelet count, reticulocyte count, and peripheral blood smear (PBS) examination.
*   In the <span style="color:red">History of an Anemic Child</span>, diet is a "red flag" if a 1-year-old is exclusively consuming cow's milk.
*   Regarding <span style="color:red">Drug-Induced Anemia</span>, ASA/NSAIDs may cause GI bleeding, while Chloramphenicol and TMP-SMX are known to cause marrow injury.
*   In <span style="color:red">Physical Examination for Anemia</span>, the presence of tachycardia, tachypnea, and hemic murmurs signifies severe anemia or acute blood loss.
*   The <span style="color:red">MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume)</span> indicates average RBC size; the lower limit for children 2-10 years is calculated as **70 + age in years**.
*   The <span style="color:red">MCHC (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration)</span> represents the amount of hemoglobin relative to the size of the cell; it is uniquely **increased** in <span style="color:red">Hereditary Spherocytosis</span>.
*   A <span style="color:red">Normal Peripheral Blood Smear</span> shows RBCs with a central pallor occupying no more than 1/3 of the cell, roughly the size of a lymphocyte nucleus.
*   <span style="color:red">Reticulocyte Count</span> provides crucial information on the rate of red cell production; a low count indicates decreased production, while a high count indicates loss or destruction.
*   The <span style="color:red">Reticulocyte Index (Corrected Retic)</span> must be calculated in anemic patients using the formula: **Observed Retic % x (Patient Hct / 0.45)**.
*   The <span style="color:red">Absolute Reticulocyte Count</span> normal range is 40,000 to 100,000; the formula is **RBC (x10¹²) x (Retic # / 1000 RBC) x 1000**.

### INADEQUATE PRODUCTION ANEMIAS

*   In <span style="color:red">Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA)</span>, anemia in term infants solely due to dietary causes usually occurs between **9-24 months of age**.
*   Regarding <span style="color:red">Neonatal Iron Stores</span>, term infants are rarely iron deficient during the first 4-6 months because iron is recycled from high fetal Hgb; stores are depleted sooner in premature infants.
*   The <span style="color:red">Stages of Iron Deficiency</span> begin with "Pre-latent" (low ferritin only), then "Latent" (low serum iron, high TIBC), and finally "IDA" (low Hb and MCV).
*   <span style="color:red">Iron Deficiency Anemia</span> labs typically show microcytic hypochromic cells, increased RDW, high TIBC, and low ferritin; iron studies are often not required if the history is classic.
*   <span style="color:red">Treatment of IDA</span> involves 3-6 mg/kg/day of elemental iron; Hgb should increase by **1 g/dL within one month** of therapy.
*   Once Hgb normalizes in <span style="color:red">Iron Deficiency Anemia</span>, iron therapy must continue for an additional 2-3 months to replete tissue stores.
*   <span style="color:red">Megaloblastic Anemia</span> in children who drink goat's milk is typically caused by **folate (B9) deficiency**.
*   In <span style="color:red">Anemia of Chronic Disease</span>, the primary mechanism is functional iron deficiency where iron is "locked" in macrophages by cytokines, leading to low serum iron but **elevated/normal ferritin**.
*   <span style="color:red">Physiologic Anemia of Infancy</span> is a normal process occurring in the first week of life and persisting for 6-8 weeks in term infants, requiring only observation.

### BONE MARROW FAILURE SYNDROMES (PANCYTOPENIAS)

*   <span style="color:red">Severe Aplastic Anemia (SAA) Criteria</span>: Bone marrow cellularity <25% PLUS two of the following: ANC <500, Platelets <20,000, or Absolute Retic <20 x 10⁹/L.
*   The treatment of choice for <span style="color:red">Acquired Aplastic Anemia</span> with a matched sibling donor is a **Bone Marrow Transplant**, which carries a >85% survival rate.
*   In <span style="color:red">Fanconi Anemia</span>, patients often present with short stature, thumb anomalies (absent/triphalangeal), and radial malformations.
*   <span style="color:red">Dyskeratosis Congenita</span> is distinguished by a classic triad of clinical findings: reticulated skin hyperpigmentation, dystrophic nails, and oral leukoplakia.
*   <span style="color:red">Pearson Syndrome</span> is a rare mitochondrial disorder characterized by macrocytic anemia, exocrine pancreatic dysfunction, and failure to thrive.

### HEMOLYTIC ANEMIAS (INCREASED DESTRUCTION)

*   <span style="color:red">Hereditary Spherocytosis (HS)</span> is the **most common red cell membrane defect** and is usually inherited in an Autosomal Dominant (70-80%) fashion.
*   The <span style="color:red">Confirmatory Test for Hereditary Spherocytosis</span> is the **Osmotic Fragility Test (OFT)**; the definitive treatment for severe cases is a splenectomy after age 7.
*   Regarding <span style="color:red">G6PD Deficiency</span>, it is the most common enzymatic defect among Filipinos (3.9% incidence) and follows an **X-linked** inheritance pattern.
*   In <span style="color:red">G6PD Deficiency</span>, hemolysis is "episodic" and triggered by oxidative stress, such as infections, fava beans, or drugs like Nitrofurantoin and Antimalarials.
*   The <span style="color:red">Thalassemias</span> are characterized by an imbalance in globin chain production (alpha or beta), leading to precipitation of excess chains that damage the RBC membrane.
*   In <span style="color:red">Beta-Thalassemia Major</span>, patients develop "chipmunk facies" due to **bone marrow expansion** and extramedullary hematopoiesis.
*   The <span style="color:red">Most Important Complication of Thalassemia</span> treatment is **iron overload (hemosiderosis)** due to chronic transfusions, necessitating iron chelation therapy.
*   The <span style="color:red">Hallmark of Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)</span> is a **positive Direct Coombs Test**.
*   In <span style="color:red">Wilson Disease</span>, hemolytic anemia can occur due to the toxic effects of copper on the RBC membrane, often associated with Kayser-Fleischer rings.

### DISTINGUISHING ENTITIES: EXAM DIFFERENTIATORS

1.  **IDA vs. Thalassemia Trait:** Both are microcytic, but <span style="color:red">Iron Deficiency Anemia</span> has a high RDW and low ferritin, whereas Thalassemia has a normal/low RDW, high RBC count, and normal ferritin.
2.  **DBA vs. TEC:** <span style="color:red">Diamond-Blackfan Anemia</span> presents at <6 months with congenital anomalies and high ADA; <span style="color:red">TEC</span> presents at >1 year in a previously healthy child with normal ADA.
3.  **HS vs. AIHA:** Both show spherocytes on PBS, but <span style="color:red">Hereditary Spherocytosis</span> has a positive family history and positive OFT, whereas <span style="color:red">AIHA</span> has a positive Direct Coombs test.
4.  **Aplastic Anemia vs. Leukemia:** Both present with pancytopenia, but <span style="color:red">Aplastic Anemia</span> shows a "fatty," hypocellular marrow, while Leukemia shows a "packed" marrow with blasts.
5.  **Fanconi Anemia vs. SAA:** <span style="color:red">Fanconi Anemia</span> is an inherited pancytopenia with physical defects (thumbs, height); <span style="color:red">Acquired Aplastic Anemia</span> typically has a preceding trigger (drugs/virus) and no skeletal anomalies.
6.  **G6PD vs. HS:** <span style="color:red">G6PD Deficiency</span> causes acute, episodic hemolysis with "bite cells" and dark urine; <span style="color:red">Hereditary Spherocytosis</span> causes chronic hemolysis with constant splenomegaly and microspherocytes.
7.  **IDA vs. ACD:** <span style="color:red">Iron Deficiency Anemia</span> has high TIBC and low Ferritin; <span style="color:red">Anemia of Chronic Disease</span> has low/normal TIBC and high/normal Ferritin.
8.  **Megaloblastic vs. IDA:** <span style="color:red">Megaloblastic Anemia</span> is macrocytic (High MCV); <span style="color:red">IDA</span> is microcytic (Low MCV).
9.  **Folate vs. B12 Deficiency:** Both are megaloblastic, but <span style="color:red">Vitamin B12 deficiency</span> is more likely to have neurological symptoms (though both were grouped as Megaloblastic in common goats' milk context).
10. **Physiologic Anemia vs. Pathologic Anemia (Neonate):** <span style="color:red">Physiologic Anemia</span> occurs at 6-8 weeks and is asymptomatic; anemia in the first week of life is usually pathologic (blood loss/hemolysis).
11. **Spherocytes vs. Microspherocytes:** Found in both <span style="color:red">HS</span> and <span style="color:red">AIHA</span>; they represent RBCs that have lost surface area, making them fragile and lacking central pallor.
12. **Target Cells:** These are the hallmark PBS finding for <span style="color:red">Hemoglobinopathies</span> like Thalassemia and Hgb C.
13. **MCHC in HS:** A <span style="color:red">High MCHC (>36)</span> is a highly specific clue for Hereditary Spherocytosis.
14. **Mentzer Index Hint (not explicitly in text but implied):** In <span style="color:red">Thalassemia</span>, the RBC count is often high despite low Hgb, whereas in <span style="color:red">IDA</span>, the RBC count is typically low.
15. **TEC Recovery:** In the recovery phase of <span style="color:red">Transient Erythroblastopenia of Childhood</span>, the MCV may temporarily increase because of the sudden influx of large reticulocytes.
16. **Drug Triggers (Marrow):** <span style="color:red">Chloramphenicol</span> causes aplastic anemia (marrow failure), whereas <span style="color:red">Nitrofurantoin</span> causes hemolysis only in G6PD deficient patients.
17. **Hypersegmentation:** If you see **Neutrophils with >5 lobes** on PBS, the diagnosis is <span style="color:red">Megaloblastic Anemia</span>.
18. **Basophilic Stippling:** Though not highlighted as a "hallmark" in this specific text, it is associated with <span style="color:red">Lead Poisoning</span> and <span style="color:red">Thalassemia</span>.
19. **Splenectomy Timing:** In <span style="color:red">HS and Thalassemia</span>, splenectomy is generally deferred until after age 6-7 to reduce the risk of post-splenectomy sepsis.
20. **Goat Milk Anemia:** High yield exam fact: <span style="color:red">Goat's Milk</span> = Folate deficiency = Megaloblastic Anemia.
21. **X-Linked Anemias:** <span style="color:red">G6PD Deficiency</span> is X-linked; therefore, it is significantly more common in males.

<h2 align="center"><b><span style="color:red">HEMOSTASIS AND BLEEDING DISORDERS</span></b></h2>

| Topic | Feature | Details |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <b><span style="color:red">Hemostasis</span></b> | <b>Definition</b> | An active process that clots blood at injury sites while limiting clot size; prevents blood loss and maintaining blood fluidity. |
| <b><span style="color:red">Hemostasis</span></b> | <b>Components</b> | Depends on the interaction between <i>Blood Vessels (Endothelium)</i>, <i>Platelets</i>, and <i>Clotting Factors</i>. |
| <b><span style="color:red">Primary Hemostasis</span></b> | <b>Mechanism</b> | Involves platelet plug formation; defects lead to mucosal/superficial bleeding. |
| <b><span style="color:red">Secondary Hemostasis</span></b> | <b>Mechanism</b> | Involves the coagulation cascade and fibrin meshwork formation; defects lead to deep tissue bleeding. |

### <b><span style="color:red">THE COAGULATION CASCADE & SCREENING</span></b>

* <b><span style="color:red">Partial Thromboplastin Time (PTT)</span></b> measures the <b>Intrinsic Pathway</b> (Factors XII, XI, IX, VIII) and the <b>Common Pathway</b> (I, II, V, X).
* <b><span style="color:red">Prothrombin Time (PT)</span></b> measures the <b>Extrinsic Pathway</b> (Factor VII) and the <b>Common Pathway</b> (I, II, V, X).
* <b><span style="color:red">PTT Prolongation with Bleeding</span></b> usually indicates a deficiency in <b>Factors VIII, IX, or XI</b>.
* <b><span style="color:red">PTT Prolongation without Bleeding</span></b> suggests a deficiency in <b>Factor XII, Prekallikrein (PreK), or High Molecular Weight Kinogen (HMWK)</b>.
* <b><span style="color:red">Isolated Prolonged PT</span></b> specifically suggests a <b>Factor VII deficiency</b>.
* <b><span style="color:red">The Common Pathway</span></b> involves <b>Factors X, V, II (Prothrombin), and I (Fibrinogen)</b>; Factor Xa activates prothrombin to thrombin.
* <b><span style="color:red">Factor XIII</span></b> is required to crosslink and stabilize the <b>Fibrin Clot</b>; it is <b>NOT</b> measured by standard PT or PTT.
* <b><span style="color:red">Vitamin K Dependent Factors</span></b> include <b>Factors II, VII, IX, and X</b> (Mnemonic: 1972).

### <b><span style="color:red">DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS BY LAB SCREEN</span></b>

| Lab Finding | Potential Diagnosis |
| :--- | :--- |
| <b><span style="color:red">↑ PT, Normal PTT/Plt</span></b> | Factor VII deficiency, Early liver disease, Vitamin K deficiency, Warfarin use. |
| <b><span style="color:red">↑ PTT, Normal PT/Plt</span></b> | Hemophilia (A or B), Factor XI or XII deficiency, von Willebrand Disease (VWD), PTT inhibitor. |
| <b><span style="color:red">↑ PT, ↑ PTT, Normal Plt</span></b> | Vitamin K deficiency, Liver disease, Massive transfusion, Oral anticoagulants, Common pathway deficiency (II, V, X, I). |
| <b><span style="color:red">↑ PT, ↑ PTT, ↓ Platelet</span></b> | <b>DIC (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation)</b> or Severe liver dysfunction. |
| <b><span style="color:red">Normal PT, Normal PTT, ↓ Platelet</span></b> | <b>ITP (Immune Thrombocytopenia)</b>, Infection, Bone marrow failure, WAS, BSS. |
| <b><span style="color:red">All Labs Normal</span></b> | <b>VWD (Mild)</b>, Platelet function disorders, Factor XIII deficiency, Vitamin C deficiency, Collagen disorders. |

### <b><span style="color:red">CLINICAL EVALUATION OF BLEEDING</span></b>

* <b><span style="color:red">Petechiae</span></b> are skin bleeds <b>< 2 mm</b> in size, usually signifying low platelet counts.
* <b><span style="color:red">Purpura</span></b> are skin bleeds between <b>2 mm and 1 cm</b>.
* <b><span style="color:red">Ecchymoses</span></b> (bruises) are skin bleeds <b>> 1 cm</b>.
* <b><span style="color:red">Hematomas</span></b> are bleeds <b>> 1 cm</b> that usually involve deep subcutaneous tissue.
* <b><span style="color:red">Mucosal Bleeding</span></b> (Epistaxis, Menorrhagia, Petechiae) is the clinical phenotype of <b>Platelet/Primary Hemostasis</b> disorders.
* <b><span style="color:red">Deep Tissue Bleeding</span></b> (Hemarthrosis, Hematoma, Delayed surgical bleeding) is the clinical phenotype of <b>Coagulation Factor/Secondary Hemostasis</b> disorders.

### <b><span style="color:red">HEMOPHILIA (A & B)</span></b>

| Feature | <b><span style="color:red">Hemophilia A</span></b> | <b><span style="color:red">Hemophilia B</span></b> |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <b>Deficiency</b> | <b>Factor VIII</b> | <b>Factor IX</b> |
| <b>Frequency</b> | Most Common (80-85%) | Less Common (15-20%) |
| <b>Inheritance</b> | <b>X-linked recessive</b> | <b>X-linked recessive</b> |
| <b>Hallmark PE</b> | <b>Hemarthrosis</b> (Joint bleeding) | <b>Hemarthrosis</b> (Joint bleeding) |
| <b>Screening Labs</b> | <b>↑ PTT</b>, Normal PT, Normal Platelets | <b>↑ PTT</b>, Normal PT, Normal Platelets |
| <b>Definitive Test</b> | <b>Factor VIII Assay</b> (VWF is normal) | <b>Factor IX Assay</b> |

* <b><span style="color:red">Hemophilia Severity</span></b> is classified by factor activity: <b>Severe (< 1%)</b> involves spontaneous bleeds; <b>Moderate (1-5%)</b> involves bleeds with mild trauma; <b>Mild (5-40%)</b> involves bleeds only with major trauma/surgery.
* <b><span style="color:red">Hemarthrosis in Hemophilia</span></b> most commonly occurs in hinged joints: <b>ankle, knees, and elbows</b>.
* <b><span style="color:red">Mixing Study</span></b> distinguishes factor deficiency from inhibitors; if the <b>PTT corrects</b> with normal plasma, it indicates a <b>Factor Deficiency</b>.
* <b><span style="color:red">Hemophilia A Treatment Dosage</span></b>: 1 unit/kg of Factor VIII raises plasma levels by <b>2% (2 IU/dl)</b>; the half-life is 8-12 hours.
* <b><span style="color:red">Hemophilia B Treatment Dosage</span></b>: 1 unit/kg of Factor IX raises plasma levels by <b>1% (1 IU/dl)</b>; the half-life is 18-24 hours.
* <b><span style="color:red">Management Principle</span></b>: For suspected life-threatening bleeds (e.g., ICH), <b>treat first (Even IF IN DOUBT)</b> before performing diagnostic tests.
* <b><span style="color:red">Supportive Care (RICE)</span></b>: Rest, Ice, Compression, and Elevation are standard for acute bleeding episodes.
* <b><span style="color:red">Hemophilia Precautions</span></b>: Patients must <b>avoid IM injections</b>, aspirin, and NSAIDs.

### <b><span style="color:red">VON WILLEBRAND DISEASE (VWD)</span></b>

* <b><span style="color:red">Von Willebrand Disease (VWD)</span></b> is the <b>most common inherited bleeding disorder</b> (1-3% of population).
* <b><span style="color:red">Von Willebrand Factor (VWF)</span></b> serves two roles: it glues <b>platelets to damaged endothelium</b> and <b>protects Factor VIII</b> from degradation.
* <b><span style="color:red">Menorrhagia</span></b> (heavy menses) is one of the <b>most important and frequent</b> complications of VWD in women.
* <b><span style="color:red">Blood Type O</span></b> individuals normally have <b>lower VWF levels</b>, which must be considered during diagnosis.
* <b><span style="color:red">VWD Type 1</span></b> is a <b>partial quantitative deficiency</b> of VWF and is the <b>most common type</b>; treated with <i>Desmopressin</i>.
* <b><span style="color:red">VWD Type 2</span></b> involves a <b>qualitative/dysfunctional</b> VWF.
* <b><span style="color:red">VWD Type 3</span></b> is the <b>total absence</b> of VWF; clinically severe and presents with low Factor VIII levels.
* <b><span style="color:red">VWF Assay</span></b> is the <b>definitive test</b> for diagnosis.

---

### <b><span style="color:red">IMMUNE THROMBOCYTOPENIA (ITP)</span></b>

* <b><span style="color:red">Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)</span></b> is the <b>most common cause of thrombocytopenia in children</b>.
* <b><span style="color:red">ITP Pathogenesis</span></b> involves <b>autoantibodies (IgG)</b> against platelet surface glycoproteins (GP IIb/IIIa or GP Ib/IX), leading to splenic destruction.
* <b><span style="color:red">Clinical Presentation of ITP</span></b> is an <b>abrupt onset of petechiae/bruising</b> in an <b>otherwise healthy/well</b> child (toddler/school age).
* <b><span style="color:red">ITP History</span></b> often includes a <b>viral infection or immunization</b> 1-2 weeks prior to symptoms.
* <b><span style="color:red">Physical Exam in ITP</span></b> should be <b>negative for splenomegaly or lymphadenopathy</b>; presence of these "red flags" suggests leukemia or other causes.
* <b><span style="color:red">ITP Diagnosis</span></b> is largely a <b>diagnosis of exclusion</b>; CBC shows isolated thrombocytopenia (< 100 x 10⁹/L) with <b>normal to large platelets</b>.
* <b><span style="color:red">Bone Marrow Examination</span></b> is <b>not required for typical ITP</b> but is used to rule out other causes if features are atypical or if steroids fail.
* <b><span style="color:red">ITP Management</span></b>: Most cases (80%) resolve spontaneously within 3-6 months; <b>observation</b> is preferred for mild bleeding.
* <b><span style="color:red">Acute ITP Pharmacotherapy</span></b>: If treatment is needed, <i>short-course corticosteroids</i>, <i>IVIG</i>, or <i>Anti-D</i> are used.
* <b><span style="color:red">Splenectomy</span></b> is reserved for <b>severe persistent or chronic ITP</b> cases.

### <b><span style="color:red">OTHER HEMOSTATIC DISORDERS</span></b>

* <b><span style="color:red">Thrombosis in Children</span></b> is rare compared to adults and usually <b>secondary</b> to critically ill states, cancer, or estrogen use.
* <b><span style="color:red">Late Vitamin K Deficiency</span></b> occurs after the neonatal period, often in <b>breastfed infants</b> or those with <b>malabsorption/liver disease</b>.
* <b><span style="color:red">DIC (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation)</span></b> is a <b>thrombotic microangiopathy</b> causing consumption of clotting factors and platelets.
* <b><span style="color:red">DIC Diagnosis</span></b> shows <b>prolonged PT, prolonged PTT, and low Platelets</b> (all deranged).
* <b><span style="color:red">DIC Treatment</span></b>: The priority is <b>treating the underlying trigger</b> (infection/malignancy) and correcting shock/acidosis.
* <b><span style="color:red">Tranexamic Acid</span></b> is an <b>antifibrinolytic</b> used to manage mucosal bleeding (epistaxis, oral bleeds) in various disorders.

### <b><span style="color:red">COMPARISON AND DIFFERENTIATION FOR EXAMS</span></b>

* <b><span style="color:red">ITP vs. Leukemia</span></b>: <b>ITP</b> presents in a well-appearing child with isolated low platelets; <b>Leukemia</b> presents with an unwell child, weight loss, bone pain, and hepatosplenomegaly.
* <b><span style="color:red">Hemophilia vs. VWD</span></b>: <b>Hemophilia</b> presents with deep tissue bleeds (hemarthrosis) and is X-linked (males); <b>VWD</b> presents with mucosal bleeds (menorrhagia/epistaxis) and affects both sexes.
* <b><span style="color:red">Hemophilia A vs. Hemophilia B</span></b>: Indistinguishable by symptoms; <b>Hemophilia A</b> is Factor VIII deficiency and common; <b>Hemophilia B</b> is Factor IX deficiency and less common.
* <b><span style="color:red">Primary vs. Secondary Hemostasis</span></b>: <b>Primary</b> (Platelets) causes immediate bleeding/petechiae; <b>Secondary</b> (Factors) causes delayed/deep bleeding and large hematomas.
* <b><span style="color:red">PT vs. PTT</span></b>: <b>PT</b> is the best screen for Factor VII and Warfarin monitoring; <b>PTT</b> is the best screen for the intrinsic pathway (8, 9, 11, 12) and Heparin monitoring.
* <b><span style="color:red">VWD vs. Hemophilia A Lab Check</span></b>: Both can show low Factor VIII, but <b>VWD</b> also has low VWF levels, whereas <b>Hemophilia A</b> has normal VWF.
* <b><span style="color:red">ITP Platelet Size</span></b>: In <b>ITP</b>, platelets are normal to large; small platelets may suggest Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome (WAS).
* <b><span style="color:red">Vitamin K Deficiency vs. Liver Disease</span></b>: Both prolong PT/PTT; <b>Vitamin K deficiency</b> usually responds to Vitamin K administration, while <b>Liver disease</b> may not and often includes low platelets.
* <b><span style="color:red">Mixing Study Correction</span></b>: If PTT <b>corrects</b>, it's a deficiency; if it <b>does not correct</b>, an inhibitor (like Lupus anticoagulant or anti-factor VIII) is present.
* <b><span style="color:red">Factor VIII vs. Factor IX Response</span></b>: 1 unit/kg of <b>Factor VIII</b> provides double the percentage rise (2%) compared to <b>Factor IX</b> (1%).
* <b><span style="color:red">VWD Type 1 vs. Type 3</span></b>: <b>Type 1</b> is mild and quantitative; <b>Type 3</b> is severe with near-total VWF absence and very low Factor VIII.
* <b><span style="color:red">Petechiae vs. Ecchymosis</span></b>: <b>Petechiae</b> are pin-point (< 2mm); <b>Ecchymoses</b> are large bruises (> 1cm).
* <b><span style="color:red">Hemostatsis Components</span></b>: <b>Platelet problems</b> affect number or function; <b>Clotting factor problems</b> involve deficiency or inhibitors; <b>Vessel problems</b> involve connective tissue or injury.
* <b><span style="color:red">Initial Screen for Bleeding Disorder</span></b>: Always includes CBC with Platelets, PT, and PTT.
* <b><span style="color:red">Bleeding Phenotype of VWD</span></b>: Characterized by <b>Easy bruising, Epistaxis, and Menorrhagia</b> (Platelet-like bleeding).
* <b><span style="color:red">Treatment Priority in DIC</span></b>: You must <b>treat the underlying cause</b>; factor replacement is only supportive.
* <b><span style="color:red">Factor Assay</span></b>: This is the <b>confirmatory test</b> for Hemophilia after a prolonged PTT is found.
* <b><span style="color:red">Treatment Priority in Hemophilia</span></b>: Bleeds must be treated <b>within 2 hours</b>; head injuries require <b>immediate</b> factor infusion even before imaging.
* <b><span style="color:red">ITP "Well Child" Rule</span></b>: If the child has significant petechiae but feels fine and the rest of the physical exam is normal, <b>ITP</b> is the top differential.
* <b><span style="color:red">Antifibrinolytic Contraindication</span></b>: While generally used for mucosal bleeds, they are usually avoided in <b>hematuria</b> due to risk of obstructive clots.

### <b>GENERAL ONCOLOGY AND CHEMOTHERAPY</b>

- Childhood cancer comprises mostly of <font color="red">leukemias, lymphomas, and brain tumors</font>, whereas carcinomas and melanomas are more characteristically seen in adolescents.
- In <font color="red">childhood malignancies</font>, the most common type of cancer overall is leukemia.
- In <font color="red">Leukemia or Neuroblastoma with bone marrow infiltration</font>, patients often present with fever, persistent infections, and neutropenia.
- In <font color="red">Lymphomas (Hodgkin and Non-Hodgkin)</font>, hallmark systemic findings include Fever of Unknown Origin (FUO), weight loss, night sweats, and painless lymphadenopathy.
- In <font color="red">Brain tumors</font>, common manifestations include headaches and visual disturbances resulting from increased intracranial pressure.
- In <font color="red">Retinoblastoma</font>, the signature clinical finding is leukokoria (white pupillary reflex).
- In <font color="red">Neuroblastoma</font>, periorbital ecchymosis ("raccoon eyes") is often present, representing metastatic disease.
- In <font color="red">Anterior mediastinal masses</font> (such as gest cell tumors and lymphomas), patients often present with cough, stridor, and tracheobronchial compression.
- In <font color="red">Neuroblastoma</font>, gastrointestinal symptoms can include a palpable abdominal mass or diarrhea.
- In <font color="red">Osteosarcoma and Ewing’s sarcoma</font>, musculoskeletal symptoms like bone pain, limping, and arthralgia are primary features.
- In <font color="red">Brain tumors, Craniopharyngioma, or Langerhans cell histiocytosis</font>, neuroendocrine involvement may manifest as diabetes insipidus and poor growth.
- At <font color="red">Cisplatin (Cp)</font>, the primary chemotherapy side effects are ototoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and high emetic potential.
- At <font color="red">Bleomycin (B)</font>, the most concerning chemotherapy-related toxicity involves lung disturbances (pulmonary fibrosis).
- At <font color="red">Doxorubicin (D)</font>, the dose-limiting chemotherapy side effect is cardiac toxicity.
- At <font color="red">Methotrexate (Mtx)</font>, chemotherapy side effects commonly include liver toxicity.
- At <font color="red">Irinotecan (Ir)</font>, a major chemotherapy side effect is severe diarrhea.

### <b>ACUTE LYMPHOBLASTIC LEUKEMIA (ALL)</b>

- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, the disease represents the most common malignant neoplasm in childhood, accounting for 31% of all childhood malignancies.
- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, the peak age of incidence is 2 to 3 years of age.
- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, the B-cell immunophenotype is more common (85%) and carries a better prognosis compared to the T-cell phenotype (15%).
- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, hyperdiploidy (extra chromosomes) is associated with a better prognosis, while hypodiploidy is unfavorable.
- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, the translocation t(12;21) (p13;q22) ETV-RUNX1 is the most common and carries a favorable prognosis.
- In <font color="red">ALL Morphology L1 Type</font>, lymphoblasts are small, uniform, and monotonous with scant cytoplasm; this is the most common type in children.
- In <font color="red">ALL Morphology L2 Type</font>, lymphoblasts are large and heterogeneous, often variable in size and shape, and can be mistaken for AML.
- In <font color="red">ALL Morphology L3 (Burkitt Type)</font>, cells show deeply basophilic cytoplasm with characteristic cytoplasmic vacuoles and round nuclei.
- At <font color="red">ALL risk stratification</font>, Standard Risk is defined as age 1–9.99 years and a initial WBC &lt; 50,000/µL.
- At <font color="red">ALL risk stratification</font>, High Risk is defined as age &lt;1 year or ≥10 years, and/or an initial WBC &gt; 50,000/µL.
- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, boys historically had a poorer prognosis due to risks of testicular relapse and higher T-cell ALL incidence, though modern intensive therapy has narrowed this gap.
- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, the definitive diagnosis requires a Bone Marrow Aspiration (BMA) showing &gt;25% lymphoblasts.
- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, Serum LDH is measured to assess tumor burden; higher levels indicate greater burden.
- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, a Chest X-ray must be performed to check for an anterior mediastinal mass.
- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, a Lumbar Puncture is required after diagnosis to assess for CNS involvement.
- In <font color="red">ALL Chemotherapy phase: Induction</font>, the goal is the induction of remission.
- In <font color="red">ALL Chemotherapy phase: Consolidation</font>, the goal is to reinforce remission in the CNS and marrow compartments.
- In <font color="red">ALL Chemotherapy phase: Interim Maintenance and Delayed Intensification</font>, the goal is to further reduce residual leukemia in the marrow.
- In <font color="red">ALL Chemotherapy phase: Maintenance</font>, the goal is to reduce the overall risk of relapse; total treatment typically takes 2-3 years.
- In <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)</font>, supportive care includes "Double Hydration," allopurinol for uric acid, and *Pneumocystis jirovecii* prophylaxis.

### <b>ACUTE MYELOGENOUS LEUKEMIA (AML)</b>

- In <font color="red">Acute Myelogenous Leukemia (AML)</font>, the relative frequency increases significantly during adolescence, representing 36% of leukemias in 15-19 year olds.
- In <font color="red">Acute Myelogenous Leukemia (AML)</font>, risk factors include ionizing radiation, Down syndrome, Fanconi anemia, and prior exposure to alkylating agents or epipodophyllotoxins.
- In <font color="red">Acute Promyelocytic Leukemia (APL)</font>, signs and laboratory findings of Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) are common.
- In <font color="red">Acute Myelogenous Leukemia (AML)</font>, "blueberry muffin" lesions (subcutaneous nodules) may be observed.
- In <font color="red">AML (especially Monocytic Subtype)</font>, infiltration of the gingiva (gums) is a characteristic clinical finding.
- In <font color="red">Acute Myelogenous Leukemia (AML)</font>, Chloromas or Granulocytic Sarcomas (leukemic masses) can fill soft tissue spaces in the eyes, gums, or skin.
- In <font color="red">Acute Myelogenous Leukemia (AML)</font>, diagnosis is confirmed by Bone Marrow Aspiration showing &gt;20% blasts.
- In <font color="red">Acute Myelogenous Leukemia (AML)</font>, favorable cytogenetic features include t(8;21), t(15;17), and Inv (16).
- In <font color="red">Acute Myelogenous Leukemia (AML)</font>, unfavorable cytogenetic features include Monosomies 7 and 5, and 11q23 abnormalities.

### <b>CHRONIC (CML) AND JUVENILE MYELOMONOCYTIC LEUKEMIA (JMML)</b>

- In <font color="red">Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia (CML)</font>, 99% of cases are associated with the t(9;22)(q34;q11) translocation, known as the Philadelphia chromosome.
- In <font color="red">Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia (CML)</font>, patients often present with hyperleukocytosis (WBC &gt;100,000) and normal or elevated platelet counts.
- In <font color="red">CML Treatment</font>, Imatinib (or Dasatinib) is an oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor that inhibits BCR-ABL and often requires lifelong administration.
- In <font color="red">Juvenile Myelomonocytic Leukemia (JMML)</font>, the disease typically affects children younger than 2 years of age and presents with massive splenomegaly, monocytosis, and no Philadelphia chromosome.
- In <font color="red">Juvenile Myelomonocytic Leukemia (JMML)</font>, mutations often lead to activation of the RAS oncogene pathway (NF1, PTPN11).
- In <font color="red">Juvenile Myelomonocytic Leukemia (JMML)</font>, transplant is the only curative intent treatment, though survival is generally very poor.

### <b>LYMPHOMAS</b>

- In <font color="red">Lymphoma</font>, it is the most common cancer in adolescents aged 15-19 years.
- In <font color="red">Hodgkin Lymphoma (HL)</font>, the hallmark finding is the Reed-Sternberg cell, described as a large cell with multiple or multilobulated nuclei.
- In <font color="red">Hodgkin Lymphoma (HL)</font>, physical examination typically reveals painless, non-tender, firm, and "rubbery" cervical or supraclavicular lymphadenopathy.
- At <font color="red">Hodgkin Lymphoma (HL) "B symptoms"</font>, the triad consists of unexplained fever &gt;38°C, weight loss &gt;10% in 6 months, and drenching night sweats.
- In <font color="red">Hodgkin Lymphoma (HL) staging</font>, Stage I involves a single node region, while Stage IV indicates diffuse metastasis to extralymphatic organs.
- In <font color="red">Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma (NHL)</font>, the disease is categorised by high-grade, aggressive behavior; 70% of pediatric patients have Stage III-IV disease at diagnosis.
- In <font color="red">Burkitt Lymphoma (NHL Subtype)</font>, characteristics include t(8;14) translocation, mature B-cell phenotype (CD19, CD20 positive), and a "starry sky" appearance (suggested by rapid turnover).
- In <font color="red">Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma (NHL)</font>, the disease frequently presents as an oncologic emergency like Superior Vena Caval (SVC) Syndrome or Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS) due to rapid cell turnover.
- In <font color="red">NHL St. Jude Staging</font>, any primary intrathoracic mass (mediastinal, pleural) is automatically classified as Stage III.
- In <font color="red">Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma (NHL)</font>, radiation therapy is reserved for special circumstances like CNS involvement in LBL, airway obstruction (SMS), or paraplegia.

### <b>ONCOLOGIC EMERGENCIES (HYPERLEUKOCYTOSIS & TLS)</b>

- At <font color="red">Hyperleukocytosis</font>, the condition is defined as a total WBC count greater than 100,000 cells/mm³.
- In <font color="red">Hyperleukocytosis Management</font>, aggressive hydration with 3L/m²/day of IVF (D5 0.45 NaCl) is required, and transfusion of Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBC) must be avoided to prevent further increasing viscosity.
- In <font color="red">Hyperleukocytosis Management</font>, platelets should ideally be maintained at 50,000 to prevent intracranial hemorrhage.
- At <font color="red">Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)</font>, the condition involves the rapid release of intracellular metabolites (Potassium, Phosphorus, Nucleic acids) exceeding renal excretory capacity.
- In <font color="red">Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)</font>, diagnosis requires two or more of: Uric acid &gt;8 mg/dL, Potassium &gt;6 meq/dL, Phosphorus &gt;2.1 mmol/L, or Calcium &lt;1.75 mmol/L (or 25% change from baseline).
- In <font color="red">Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)</font>, hyperkalemia is characterized by widened QRS and peaked T-waves on ECG.
- In <font color="red">Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)</font>, hypocalcemia presents with tetany, seizures, a prolonged QTc interval, and + Trousseau/Chovestek signs.
- In <font color="red">Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS) Management</font>, alkalinization of urine is now avoided to prevent precipitation of xanthine calculi and calcium phosphate in renal tubules.
- At <font color="red">Rasburicase (recombinant urate oxidase)</font>, the drug effectively reduces existing uric acid (unlike Allopurinol which only blocks new formation).
- In <font color="red">Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)</font>, dialysis is indicated for potassium &gt;6 meq/L, phosphate &gt;10 mg/dL, or volume overload/anuria unresponsive to medical therapy.

### <b>DIFFERENTIATION AND COMPARISONS FOR EXAMS</b>

1. In <font color="red">Acute Leukemia Diagnosis</font>, ALL requires &gt;25% lymphoblasts in the bone marrow, whereas AML requires &gt;20% blasts.
2. In <font color="red">Leukemia vs. ITP</font>, Leukemia typically presents with pancytopenia and organomegaly, while ITP presents with isolated thrombocytopenia and a normal-sized spleen.
3. In <font color="red">CML vs. JMML</font>, the Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22) is present in 99% of CML cases but is notably absent in JMML.
4. In <font color="red">CML vs. Acute Leukemia</font>, CML usually presents with extreme hyperleukocytosis but with normal or elevated platelet counts, unlike the thrombocytopenia typical of acute forms.
5. In <font color="red">Hodgkin vs. Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma</font>, Hodgkin usually presents with slow, firm, rubbery nodal enlargement, while Non-Hodgkin presents with a very rapidly progressing mass and higher risk of SVC syndrome.
6. In <font color="red">Hodgkin Lymphoma Staging</font>, Stage II refers to nodes on the same side of the diaphragm, while Stage III refers to involvement on both sides of the diaphragm.
7. In <font color="red">NHL Staging</font>, a single side of the diaphragm involvement is Stage II, but any intrathoracic/mediastinal mass is automatically Stage III.
8. In <font color="red">Hyperleukocytosis risk</font>, Myeloblasts (AML) are bigger and "stickier" than Lymphoblasts (ALL), causing leukostasis at lower counts (~200k in AML vs ~300k in ALL).
9. In <font color="red">Allopurinol vs. Rasburicase</font>, Allopurinol prevents the *formation* of new uric acid, while Rasburicase breaks down *pre-existing* uric acid.
10. In <font color="red">Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)</font>, the classic metabolic quadriad is Hyperuricemia, Hyperkalemia, Hyperphosphatemia, and *Hypo*calcemia (Secondary).
11. In <font color="red">ALL Morphology</font>, L1 cells are small and uniform (most common in children), L2 cells are large/heterogeneous (mimic AML), and L3 cells are basophilic with vacuoles (Burkitt type).
12. In <font color="red">AML special findings</font>, Gingival hyperplasia/infiltration is most specific for the monocytic subtype (M4/M5).
13. In <font color="red">Leukemia Skin findings</font>, "Blueberry muffin" lesions are associated with AML, while "Chloromas" are specifically localized collections of myeloblasts.
14. In <font color="red">ALL cytogenetics</font>, t(12;21) is the most common and "good" prognosis, while t(9;22) in ALL is associated with "poor" prognosis.
15. In <font color="red">Pediatric vs. Adult Cancers</font>, pediatric cancers are predominantly blast-based (leukemias/sarcomas), while adult cancers are predominantly epithelial (carcinomas).
16. In <font color="red">Hodgkin Lymphoma biopsy</font>, Excision biopsy is preferred over needle biopsy to allow for proper subtyping and immunostaining.
17. In <font color="red">Burkitt Lymphoma genetics</font>, the characteristic translocation is t(8;14) involving the MYC oncogene.
18. In <font color="red">ALL Support</font>, double hydration is standard, but in <font color="red">Hyperleukocytosis</font>, PRBC transfusions are avoided as they worsen blood viscosity and risk stroke.
19. In <font color="red">JMML Diagnosis</font>, the patient is usually &lt;2 years old with monocytosis; in <font color="red">CML Diagnosis</font>, the patient can be older and has the Philadelphia chromosome.
20. In <font color="red">Hyperuricemia Presentation</font>, patients may show microscopic hematuria due to uric acid crystals in the renal tubules.

* In <font color="red">Neuroblastoma</font>, periorbital hemorrhage or **"raccoon eyes"** occurs due to tumor infiltration of the skull or orbital bones.
* <font color="red">Horner’s Syndrome</font> (meiosis, ptosis, enophthalmos, anhidrosis) in <font color="red">Neuroblastoma</font> indicates cervical sympathetic chain involvement.
* <font color="red">Wilms Tumor</font> is associated with specific syndromes including **WAGR** (Wilms, Aniridia, GU anomalies, Retardation), **Denys-Drash**, and **Beckwith-Wiedemann Syndrome**.
* A <font color="red">biopsy</font> is strictly discouraged/contraindicated in <font color="red">Wilms Tumor</font> as it results in tumor seeding and upstaging of the disease.
* <font color="red">Hypertension</font> in a child with <font color="red">Wilms Tumor</font> is common and is driven by increased renin production or renal artery obstruction.
* <font color="red">Stage MS Neuroblastoma</font> is a unique category for children <18 months where metastasis is confined to skin, liver, or bone marrow and may undergo spontaneous regression.
* <font color="red">Beckwith-Wiedemann Syndrome</font> predisposes children to <font color="red">Wilms Tumor, Hepatoblastoma, and Adrenal Carcinoma</font>.
* <font color="red">Klinefelter Syndrome</font> is specifically associated with an increased risk of mediastinal <font color="red">Germ Cell Tumors</font>.
* <font color="red">AFP (Alpha-fetoprotein)</font> levels normally remain high in infants until approximately 8 months of age, which must be considered when diagnosing <font color="red">Germ Cell Tumors</font>.

### II. BRAIN TUMORS, BONE TUMORS, AND RETINOBLASTOMA

| Feature | <font color="red">Brain Tumors (General)</font> | <font color="red">Retinoblastoma</font> | <font color="red">Osteosarcoma (OS)</font> | <font color="red">Ewing Sarcoma (EWS)</font> |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Incidence** | 2nd most common pediatric cancer | Most common intraocular tumor | Most common bone tumor; Peak adolescence | 2nd most common bone tumor |
| **Key Findings** | Morning headache, vomiting, papilledema | **Leukocoria** (white reflex), Strabismus | Bone pain, swelling at metaphysis | Bone pain, swelling at diaphysis |
| **Imaging** | MRI with Gadolinium (Standard) | Chalky white-gray retinal mass | **"Sunburst"** pattern; Codman triangle | **"Onion-skinning"** (periosteal reaction) |
| **Pathology** | Location-dependent (Astrocytoma, Medulloblastoma) | Small round blue cells; **Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes** | Malignant **osteoid** production | Small round blue cells (PAS positive) |
| **Contraindication** | N/A | **Biopsy is contraindicated** | N/A | N/A |

* <font color="red">Brain tumor location</font> varies by age: In the 1st year of life, tumors are typically supratentorial; between 1-10 years, they are primarily infratentorial (e.g., Medulloblastoma); after age 10, they are again mostly supratentorial.
* <font color="red">Parinaud Syndrome</font> (upward gaze palsy, light-near dissociation/Pseudo-Argyll Robertson pupil) is a hallmark of tumors in the **pineal region**.
* <font color="red">Diencephalic Syndrome</font>, characterized by failure to thrive and emaciation despite normal intake, is seen in tumors of the **suprasellar and third ventricular regions**.
* <font color="red">Retinoblastoma</font> survivors, particularly those with hereditary forms (RB1/TP53 mutations), have a significantly increased risk of developing <font color="red">Osteosarcoma</font> later in life.
* <font color="red">Rhabdomyosarcoma</font> is the most common pediatric soft tissue sarcoma, frequently presenting as a small round blue cell tumor requiring immunohistochemistry (desmin, myogenin) for diagnosis.
* <font color="red">Botryoid Rhabdomyosarcoma</font> is a variant that appears like a **"bunch of grapes"** and is typically found in mucosal-lined cavities like the vagina or bladder.
* <font color="red">Alveolar Rhabdomyosarcoma</font> is associated with the **PAX-FOXO1 transcript** and carries the poorest prognosis among the histologic types.

### III. PEDIATRIC BLOOD TRANSFUSIONS

| Component | <font color="red">PRBC (Red Cells)</font> | <font color="red">Platelets</font> | <font color="red">FFP (Plasma)</font> |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Dose** | 10 - 15 mL/kg | 5 - 10 mL/kg (Pheresis: 1 unit/10kg) | 10 - 15 mL/kg |
| **Effect** | 10 mL/kg raises Hb by **2 g/dL** | Increases count by ~50k-100k | Replaces clotting factors |
| **Triggers** | Hb <7 g/dL in stable; Hb <10 if symptomatic | <10k (stable); <20k (sepsis/DIC); <50k (surgery) | Bleeding + PT/PTT >1.5x normal; DIC |

* The <font color="red">30-minute rule</font> in blood banking states that RBC units left at room temperature for >30 minutes cannot be returned to storage for reissue.
* The <font color="red">4-hour rule</font> requires that the infusion of any blood component must be completed within 4 hours of removal from controlled storage to prevent bacterial proliferation.
* In <font color="red">pediatric blood transfusion</font>, the only compatible IV fluid to be given concomitantly with PRBCs is **0.9% Normal Saline**.
* <font color="red">Fever</font> is NOT a contraindication to starting a blood transfusion; however, a temperature rise of >1°C during/after is classified as a <font color="red">Febrile Non-hemolytic Transfusion Reaction</font>.
* <font color="red">Emergency Release of Blood</font> (when group is unknown) should utilize **Type O RBCs** and **Type AB Platelets/Plasma**.
* <font color="red">Pediatric Massive Transfusion</font> is defined as replacing >50% of total blood volume in 3 hours or >100% in 24 hours.
* Routine <font color="red">premedication</font> (Acetaminophen/Diphenhydramine) for blood transfusions is generally considered a **poor practice** as evidence suggests it is not effective prophylaxis for first-time reactions.
* In <font color="red">Hema-Oncology</font> patients, a <font color="red">Lumbar Puncture</font> usually requires a platelet threshold of **20k-40k/uL**.
* For <font color="red">Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)</font>, transfusion should **never be withheld** if life-threatening anemia is present, even if crossmatching is incompatible.

### IV. DIFFERENTIATING CLINICAL PEARLS (EXAM FOCUS)

1. <font color="red">Neuroblastoma vs. Wilms Tumor</font>: Neuroblastoma occurs in younger children (<1 yr), often **crosses the midline**, and has calcifications on CT; Wilms occurs older (2-3 yrs), remains in the **renal flank (rarely crosses midline)**, and shows the "Claw Sign."
2. <font color="red">Osteosarcoma vs. Ewing Sarcoma Imaging</font>: Osteosarcoma presents with a **"Sunburst"** pattern at the metaphysis; Ewing Sarcoma presents with **"Onion-skinning"** at the diaphysis.
3. <font color="red">Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) Risk Stratification</font>: **Standard Risk** is age 1-10 years and WBC <50,000; **High Risk** is age <1 year or >10 years, or WBC >50,000.
4. <font color="red">AFP vs. B-HCG Markers</font>: AFP is elevated in **Yolk Sac Tumors and Hepatoblastoma**; B-HCG is elevated in **Choriocarcinoma**.
5. <font color="red">Supratentorial vs. Infratentorial Brain Tumors</font>: Infratentorial tumors (Medulloblastoma/Astrocytoma) dominate the **1-10 year old age group**; Supratentorial tumors dominate **infancy and adolescence**.
6. <font color="red">Small Round Blue Cells</font>: This is a common histologic finding for **Neuroblastoma, Ewing Sarcoma, Rhabdomyosarcoma, and Retinoblastoma**; differentiation requires IHC (e.g., desmin for Rhabdo, PAS for Ewing).
7. <font color="red">Thrombocytosis in Tumors</font>: If an abdominal mass is present, thrombocytosis points toward **Hepatoblastoma**; if pancytopenia is present, it suggests bone marrow infiltration (likely **Neuroblastoma** or Leukemia).
8. <font color="red">Tumor Marker "Normal Value" Trap</font>: In **Teratomas and Germinomas**, tumor markers (AFP/HCG) are typically **Normal**; do not rule them out based on markers alone.
9. <font color="red">Claw Sign vs. Midline Crossing</font>: The **Claw Sign** is specific for an intra-renal tumor (**Wilms**); **encasing major vessels** and crossing the midline is classic for **Neuroblastoma**.
10. <font color="red">Leukocoria (White Reflex)</font>: While the differential is broad, in pediatric oncology, this is **Retinoblastoma** until proven otherwise. 
11. <font color="red">Opsoclonus-Myoclonus ("Dancing Eyes/Feet")</font>: Although less emphasized in this text, it is a paraneoplastic syndrome highly associated with **Neuroblastoma**.
12. <font color="red">Renin elevation</font>: This is the primary cause of hypertension in **Wilms Tumor**, distinguishing it from catecholamine-induced hypertension in **Neuroblastoma**.
13. <font color="red">WAGR Syndrome</font>: The "A" stands for **Aniridia** (absence of iris), which is a major red flag for underlying **Wilms Tumor**.
14. <font color="red">Botryoid variant</font>: If the question mentions a "bunch of grapes" in a child's vagina or bladder, the answer is **Embryonal Rhabdomyosarcoma**.
15. <font color="red">PRBC Volume Calculation</font>: Always remember **10 mL/kg raises Hb by 2 g/dL**; this is a frequent calculation on boards.
16. <font color="red">VMA/HVA vs. Hematuria</font>: Urine **catecholamines (VMA)** are for **Neuroblastoma**; **Hematuria** is for **Wilms**.
17. <font color="red">Most Common Malignancy</font>: **Leukemia** is #1 overall; **Brain Tumors** are #2 overall; **Neuroblastoma** is the #1 **extracranial solid** tumor.
18. <font color="red">Age-Specific Findings</font>: If a mass is found in a **neonate**, it is likely a **congenital malformation**; if found at **age 2**, it is likely **Wilms**.
19. <font color="red">Pancytopenia in Solid Tumors</font>: In **Neuroblastoma**, 75% are metastatic at diagnosis, often involving the bone marrow, causing **pancytopenia** similar to leukemia.
20. <font color="red">Parinaud Syndrome pupil</font>: In **Pineal tumors**, the pupil reacts to **accommodation but not to light** (Pseudo-Argyll Robertson pupil).

| **Objective Data** | <font color="red">Initial findings</font> that serve as a baseline for stabilization include vital signs, urine output, and sensorium. |

| **Secondary Assessment** | **S-A-M-P-L-E** | Focuses on a resuscitation-oriented history: <font color="red">Signs/Symptoms</font>, <font color="red">Allergies</font>, <font color="red">Medications</font>, <font color="red">Past medical history</font>, <font color="red">Last meal</font>, and <font color="red">Events preceding illness</font>. |
| **Tertiary Assessment** | **Diagnostics** | Includes <font color="red">Laboratory tests</font>, <font color="red">Radiologic imaging</font>, and other specialized diagnostics to confirm the working impression. |

### PRIMARY ASSESSMENT: AIRWAY AND BREATHING DETAILS

| Condition type | Physical Findings |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Upper Airway Obstruction (UAO)** | Characterized by <font color="red">stridor</font>; common causes include Croup, Epiglottitis, or foreign body. |
| **Lower Airway Obstruction (LAO)** | Characterized by <font color="red">wheezing</font>; common causes include Asthma and Bronchiolitis. |
| **Lung Parenchymal Disease (LPD)** | Characterized by <font color="red">crackles (rales)</font>; common causes include Pneumonia and Pulmonary Edema. |
| **Disordered Control of Breathing (DCB)** | Characterized by <font color="red">abnormal breathing patterns or no breath sounds</font> due to neurologic issues like TBI, GBS, or drug overdose. |

*   An <font color="red">Airway</font> is classified as **Clear** (no intervention), **Maintainable** (needs positioning/suctioning), or **Unmaintainable** (needs intubation).
*   The <font color="red">minimum parameters for Breathing assessment</font> are: 
    1. Rate of breathing
    2. Effort of breathing
    3. Air entry/Breath sounds
    4. Oxygen saturation
*   In <font color="red">Respiratory Distress</font>, signs include increased work of breathing, nasal flaring, head bobbing, grunting, and retractions.
*   <font color="red">Respiratory Failure</font> occurs when the body's metabolic demand for oxygen is not met by breathing, eventually leading to respiratory arrest.

### PRIMARY ASSESSMENT: CIRCULATION, DISABILITY, AND EXPOSURE

| System | Parameters / Scale | Key Diagnostic Points |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Circulation** | **5 Minimums** | Includes <font color="red">Heart rate</font>, <font color="red">Blood pressure</font>, <font color="red">Central/Peripheral pulses</font>, <font color="red">Capillary refill time (CRT)</font>, and <font color="red">Urine output</font>. |
| **Disability** | **AVPU** | Measures level of consciousness: <font color="red">Alert</font>, Responds to <font color="red">Voice</font>, Responds to <font color="red">Pain</font>, or <font color="red">Unconscious</font>. |
| **Disability** | **HGT/Glucose** | <font color="red">Blood sugar check</font> is a mandatory part of assessing neurologic disability in a critical child. |
| **Exposure** | **Visual Survey** | Immediate check for <font color="red">Fever (>37.8 C)</font>, <font color="red">Rashes</font>, or <font color="red">Bruises</font>. |

*   <font color="red">Compensated Shock</font> manifests with signs of poor perfusion (tachycardia, delayed CRT) but maintaining a **normal systolic BP** for age.
*   <font color="red">Hypotensive Shock</font> is characterized by impaired perfusion and a **systolic BP below the lower limit** for age.

### MANAGEMENT OF RESPIRATORY DISTRESS AND FAILURE

| Device / Intervention | Specification | Details |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Oxygen: Nasal Cannula** | **0.25 – 4 L/min** | Used for mild oxygen requirements. |
| **Oxygen: Simple Mask** | **5 – 10 L/min** | Moderate delivery of FiO2. |
| **Oxygen: Non-Rebreathing Mask** | **11 – 15 L/min** | Highest FiO2 for non-invasive delivery; used in shock or severe distress. |
| **Maneuver: Head Tilt-Chin Lift** | **Non-Trauma** | Preferred for opening the airway in victims without suspected cervical spine injury. |
| **Maneuver: Jaw Thrust** | **Trauma** | Mandatory for opening the airway when <font color="red">cervical spine injury</font> is suspected. |
| **Rescue Breaths** | **Rate** | Give <font color="red">20 to 30 breaths per minute</font> (1 breath every 2-3 seconds) for a child with a pulse but inadequate breathing. |

*   The <font color="red">Oropharyngeal Airway (OPA)</font> is used ONLY for unconscious patients without a gag reflex; size is measured from the **corner of the mouth to the angle of the mandible**.
*   The <font color="red">Nasopharyngeal Airway (NPA)</font> is for conscious or semi-conscious patients with a gag reflex; size is measured from the **tragus of the ear to the tip of the nose**.
*   <font color="red">NPA Contraindications</font> include basal skull fractures (potential cranial entry) or coagulation defects.
*   The <font color="red">“C-E” grip</font> is the standard technique for Bag-Mask Ventilation (BMV) to ensure a tight seal and jaw lift.
*   In <font color="red">Bag-Mask Ventilation</font>, the bag should only be squeezed enough to see chest rise; over-squeezing can cause barotrauma (pneumothorax).

---

### ENDOTRACHEAL INTUBATION (ETT)

*   <font color="red">Endotracheal Intubation</font> is the most secure airway method; indications include GCS < 8, failure to oxygenate/ventilate, or anticipatory (e.g., GBS).
*   The <font color="red">SOAP MM mnemonic</font> for intubation preparation stands for:
    1. **S**uction
    2. **O**xygen
    3. **A**irway (equipment/size)
    4. **P**eople
    5. **M**onitor
    6. **M**edications
*   The <font color="red">Formula for Cuffed ETT size</font> is **(Age in Years / 4) + 3.5**.
*   The <font color="red">Formula for Uncuffed ETT size</font> is **(Age in Years / 4) + 4**.
*   The <font color="red">Estimated ETT Depth</font> (lip-to-tip) is calculated as **ETT Size x 3**.
*   The <font color="red">DOPE Mnemonic</font> is used to troubleshoot sudden deterioration in an intubated patient:
    *   **D**: Displacement
    *   **O**: Obstruction
    *   **P**: Pneumothorax
    *   **E**: Equipment failure

### SHOCK: TYPES AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

| Type of Shock | Primary Problem | Common Causes/Etiologies |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Hypovolemic Shock** | Inadequate blood/fluid volume | AGE (vomiting/diarrhea), hemorrhage, Nephrotic syndrome. |
| **Distributive Shock** | Inappropriate distribution (vasodilation) | Sepsis, Anaphylaxis, Neurogenic shock, Drug overdose. |
| **Cardiogenic Shock** | Impaired cardiac contractility | CHD, Arrhythmias, Myocarditis, RHD. |
| **Obstructive Shock** | Obstructed blood flow | Tension pneumothorax, Cardiac tamponade, Aortic stenosis. |

*   The <font color="red">earliest compensatory mechanism</font> for decreased cardiac output in shock is **Tachycardia**.
*   <font color="red">Septic Shock</font> is unique as it encompasses three shock types: hypovolemic (capillary leak), distributive (vasodilation via NO), and cardiogenic (inflammatory depression of contractility).
*   <font color="red">Shock Physiology</font> involves the equation: **Blood Pressure = Cardiac Output x Systemic Vascular Resistance**.
*   In <font color="red">Dissociative Shock</font> (e.g., CO poisoning), O2 delivery is impaired because O2 cannot be released from hemoglobin to tissues, despite normal circulation.

---

### MANAGEMENT OF SEPTIC SHOCK (TIME-SENSITIVE)

*   <font color="red">Initial Fluid Bolus in Shock</font> is **10-20 cc/kg** given over 15 to 20 minutes.
*   The <font color="red">Preferred Fluid Order</font> for shock resuscitation is: 
    1. **Sterofundin** (Isotonic electrolyte solution)
    2. **Acetated Ringer's** (Buffer usable directly)
    3. **Lactated Ringer's** (Liver must process buffer)
    4. **NSS** (Risk of hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis)
*   If <font color="red">IV access</font> cannot be established within the first 90 seconds, an **Intraosseous (IO) line** should be inserted immediately.
*   Signs of <font color="red">Fluid Overload/Congestion</font> to monitor during resuscitation include new-onset crackles, hepatomegaly, and edema.
*   <font color="red">Inotropes (Epinephrine/Norepinephrine)</font> are indicated if the patient remains in shock after 3 fluid boluses (fluid-resistant shock).
*   <font color="red">Hydrocortisone (5mg/kg)</font> is used in catecholamine-resistant shock to treat potential adrenal crisis/insufficiency.

### DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS AND COMPARISONS

1.  <font color="red">Upper vs. Lower Airway Obstruction</font>: Upper presents with **stridor** (inspiratory), while Lower presents with **wheezing** (expiratory).
2.  <font color="red">Compensated vs. Hypotensive Shock</font>: Compensated has **Normal SBP**, whereas Hypotensive has **Low SBP**; both show poor perfusion signs (tachycardia, delayed CRT).
3.  <font color="red">OPA vs. NPA</font>: **OPA** is for patients WITHOUT a gag reflex; **NPA** is for patients WITH a gag reflex.
4.  <font color="red">Head Tilt-Chin Lift vs. Jaw Thrust</font>: **Head Tilt** is for medical cases; **Jaw Thrust** is for suspected Trauma/C-spine injuries.
5.  <font color="red">Cuffed vs. Uncuffed ETT</font>: **Cuffed** is now generally recommended to prevent air leaks and ensure better ventilation; **Uncuffed** size formula adds +4 to age/4 while Cuffed adds +3.5.
6.  <font color="red">Hypovolemic vs. Distributive Shock Preload</font>: In **Hypovolemic**, preload is low due to loss of volume; in **Distributive**, preload is low due to "relative" hypovolemia from massive vasodilation.
7.  <font color="red">Respiratory Distress vs. Failure</font>: **Distress** includes increased effort (tachypnea, flaring); **Failure** includes inadequate gas exchange (hypoxemia, hypercarbia) and can lead to gasping or agonal breathing.
8.  <font color="red">LR vs. AR Fluids</font>: **Acetated Ringer's** is preferred in shock because the buffer is used directly; **Lactated Ringer's** requires liver perfusion to convert lactate to bicarbonate.
9.  <font color="red">Cardiac Arrest Etiology (Adult vs. Peds)</font>: Pediatric arrest is usually **Respiratory/Secondary**; Adult arrest is usually **Sudden Cardiac/Primary**.
10. <font color="red">Displacement (D) vs. Obstruction (O) in DOPE</font>: **Displacement** is the tube moving out (check with breath sounds/XR); **Obstruction** is the tube blocked by secretions or biting (check with suction catheter).
11. <font color="red">Pneumothorax vs. Ventilator Failure in DOPE</font>: **Pneumothorax** results in deterioration regardless of bagging/ventilator; **Ventilator Failure** shows improvement when switched from the machine to manual bagging.
12. <font color="red">Normal SBP Calculation (1-10 years)</font>: Calculated as **(Age x 2) + 70**; anything below this is hypotensive.
13. <font color="red">LPD vs. DCB Auscultation</font>: **Lung Parenchymal Disease** has crackles/rales; **Disordered Control of Breathing** may have entirely absent or irregular breath sounds.
14. <font color="red">Septic Shock vs. Other Distributive Shocks</font>: **Sepsis** typically presents with a history of fever/infection, whereas **Anaphylaxis** presents with allergen exposure and **Neurogenic** with trauma history.
15. <font color="red">Preload vs. Afterload Manipulation</font>: Fluid boluses increase **Preload**; Vasopressors (like Epinephrine) increase **Afterload** via vasoconstriction.
16. <font color="red">Pneumothorax vs. Gastric Distension</font>: **Pneumothorax** shows decreased breath sounds and hyper-resonance; **Gastric Distension** (from ETT in esophagus) shows gurgling in the stomach and abdominal swelling.
17. <font color="red">Inotropes vs. Steroids in Shock</font>: **Inotropes** (Epi) increase contractility/tone directly; **Steroids** (Hydrocortisone) are given if inotropes fail to address potential adrenal insufficiency.
18. <font color="red">Nasal Cannula vs. NRM Flow</font>: **Cannula** is low flow (max 4L); **NRM** is high flow (requires 11-15L to keep reservoir bag inflated).
19. <font color="red">Initial vs. Primary Assessment</font>: **Initial** is a "first-look" (ABC only); **Primary** is a hands-on examination (ABCDE with vitals).
20. <font color="red">Tachycardia Interpretation</font>: **Tachycardia** is the first sign of compensation in shock but must be differentiated from pain, fever, or medication effects (e.g., Albuterol).

# 5.6 - Neurologic Emergencies and Stabilization
## Summary

### **RAPID SEQUENCE INTUBATION (RSI) AND AIRWAY MANAGEMENT**

| Feature | Details |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Most Important Part** | Preparation of materials (Lecturer emphasis). |
| **Indications** | 1. Unable to maintain airway patency or protect against aspiration. 2. Failure to maintain adequate oxygenation. 3. Failure to control blood CO2 levels. 4. Sedation/paralysis required for a procedure. 5. Anticipated deteriorating course. |
| **Absolute Contraindication** | Known complete airway obstruction (e.g., severe subglottic stenosis); requires emergency cricothyroidotomy or tracheostomy. |
| **Difficult Airway Predictors** | Limited mouth opening, limited neck mobility, micrognathia (small jaw), short neck, or history of difficult intubation. |
| **Preoxygenation Goal** | Maintain O2 saturation >95% using 100% FiO2 for 3 mins (spontaneous) or bag-mask (apneic). |
| **Anatomical Positioning** | Sniffing position (supine); in infants/neonates, place a cloth behind shoulders to prevent head flexion due to the large occiput. |

#### **PHARMACOLOGY OF RSI**

| Drug Category | Agent | Key Flashcard Facts |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pretreatment** | **Atropine** | Given to blunt the vagal reflex (bradycardia) and reduce airway secretions; recommended for children ≤1yr, or <5yrs if receiving succinylcholine. |
| **Pretreatment** | **Lidocaine** | Given to minimize sudden increases in <span style="color:red">Intracranial Pressure (ICP)</span> during intubation. |
| **Induction (Sedative)**| **Etomidate** | Safe for hemodynamic instability; neuroprotective; do **NOT** use routinely in septic shock (causes transient adrenal suppression). |
| **Induction (Sedative)**| **Ketamine** | DOC for asthma/bronchospasm; hemodynamically stable (better for shock than midazolam); may increase oral secretions. |
| **Induction (Sedative)**| **Propofol** | Causes <span style="color:red">hypotension</span> (avoid in shock); useful for status epilepticus or stable patients with neuro problems. |
| **Induction (Sedative)**| **Midazolam** | Benzodiazepine of choice; may cause hypotension; onset takes 2-3 minutes. |
| **Analgesia** | **Fentanyl** | Most common analgesic; SE include respiratory depression and chest wall rigidity (if given too fast). |
| **Paralytic** | **Rocuronium** | DOC for muscle relaxation; ensures rapid paralysis; must insert tube immediately as breathing stops. |
| **Paralytic** | **Succinylcholine**| Rapid onset; **Contraindicated** in: hyperkalemia, burns/crush injuries (after 48-72h), malignant hyperthermia, and chronic muscle disease (Duchenne/Becker). |

- Atropine is used in <span style="color:red">Rapid Sequence Intubation</span> to prevent bradycardia and decrease secretions which might obstruct the airway.
- Lidocaine is administered during <span style="color:red">Intubation</span> to prevent the patient from straining, which would otherwise increase intracranial pressure and risk herniation.
- The <span style="color:red">Sniffing Position</span> is the gold standard for patient alignment during intubation, often requiring a shoulder roll in infants to compensate for a prominent occiput.
- The <span style="color:red">Sellick Maneuver</span> involves applying pressure to the cricoid cartilage to visualize the glottis or prevent aspiration; it is NOT a routine maneuver and is used only if visualization is difficult.
- Straight blades (Miller) are typically used in <span style="color:red">pediatric intubation</span> to address the floppy epiglottis, while curved blades (Macintosh) are more common in adults.
- Confirmation of <span style="color:red">Endotracheal Tube (ETT) placement</span> is achieved via auscultation, observation of chest rise, vital sign monitoring, and ultimately a chest X-ray.

---

### **INTRAOSSEOUS (IO) CANNULATION**

| Feature | Details |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Mechanism** | Accesses non-collapsible medullary sinuses in long bones that drain into the central circulation. |
| **Indications** | Emergency vascular access if IV line cannot be established within 90 seconds in shock. |
| **Most Common Site** | **Proximal Tibia** (1-2 cm medial and distal to the tibial tuberosity). |
| **Absolute CI** | Fractured bone, previously penetrated bone (on the same site), or vascular interruption in the extremity. |
| **Relative CI** | Cellulitis/burns at site, Osteogenesis Imperfecta (fragility), or right-to-left intracardiac shunts (embolism risk). |

- <span style="color:red">Intraosseous (IO) access</span> allows for the administration of any IV drug, resuscitation fluids, and even obtaining marrow for lab studies (electrolytes, ABG, cultures).
- Technique for <span style="color:red">IO Cannulation</span> involves directing the needle caudad (10-15 degrees away from the growth plate) using a twisting motion until a "pop" or decrease in resistance is felt.
- Confirmation of <span style="color:red">IO needle placement</span> is definitive if the needle stands firmly on its own, bone marrow is aspirated, and fluid flushes easily without subcutaneous swelling.
- <span style="color:red">Compartment Syndrome</span> is a potential complication of IO access caused by the extravasation of fluids into the subcutaneous tissue.
- Fat emboli are a rare risk of <span style="color:red">IO access</span>; practitioners are advised not to push aspirated marrow back into the cavity.

### **NEUROLOGIC EMERGENCIES AND TBI**

| Topic | Key Facts |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Dynamics** | **CPP = MAP - ICP**. Mainstay is preserving nutrient supply (Oxygen/Glucose). |
| **Compensatory Mech**| 1. Displacing CSF to spinal canal. 2. Decreasing cerebral blood volume. 3. Expanding cranial volume (infant sutures). |
| **Autoregulation** | CBF remains constant despite BP changes until limits are exceeded. Low BP = Max Dilation; High BP = Max Constriction. |
| **Secondary Injury** | Targets of neuro-ICU. Includes edema, apoptosis, and ischemia from hypotension/hypoxia following the primary impact. |
| **ICP Hallmark** | **Coma (GCS 3-8)** is the hallmark of severe TBI. Peak swelling occurs at **48-72 hours**. |
| **Herniation Triad** | (Cushing's) Hypertension, bradycardia, and irregular respirations (plus pupillary changes). |

#### **TBI MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES**

| Tier | Interventions |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1st Tier (Basic)** | Head of bed elevation (midline/30 deg), controlled ventilation (target normal CO2), sedation/analgesia, euvolemia with **Normal Saline**. |
| **Acute Herniation** | **Hyperventilation** (urgent/temporary), **Hypertonic Saline (3%)** (5-10 mL/kg), or **Mannitol** (0.25-1.0 g/kg). |
| **2nd Tier (Refractory)**| Decompressive craniectomy, Pentobarbital coma, Mild hypothermia (32-34C - controversial), extreme hyperventilation (PaCO2 25-30). |
| **Supportive Care** | BMB prophylaxis (Levetiracetam/Fosphenytoin), Avoid glucose >200 mg/dL, Avoid Hypotonic fluids (Target Na 145-155). |

- <span style="color:red">Cerebral Perfusion Pressure (CPP)</span> targets are age-dependent: 50 mmHg (2-6y), 55 mmHg (7-10y), and 65 mmHg (11-16y).
- <span style="color:red">Hyperthermia</span> must be avoided in brain injury as it increases cellular metabolic demand and worsens tissue hypoxia.
- <span style="color:red">Hypercarbia (High CO2)</span> causes cerebral vasodilation, increasing intracranial blood volume and subsequently increasing ICP.
- <span style="color:red">Modified Pediatric Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)</span> is used for children <2 years old to account for non-verbal development scores.
- <span style="color:red">Cranial CT Scanning</span> in patients <2 years old (PECARN) is indicated if GCS ≤14, there is altered mental status, or a palpable skull fracture.
- <span style="color:red">Cranial CT Scanning</span> in patients ≥2 years old is indicated if GCS ≤14, there is altered mental status, or signs of a basilar skull fracture (Raccoon eyes/Battle sign).
- <span style="color:red">Hypertonic Saline (HTS)</span> is often preferred over Mannitol for pediatric cerebral edema as it helps maintain volume while targeting high-normal sodium (145-155).
- <span style="color:red">Anticonvulsant prophylaxis</span> (e.g., Levetiracetam) prevents early post-traumatic seizures (within 1 week) but does not prevent late-onset epilepsy.

### **BASIC LIFE SUPPORT (BLS) AND CPR**

| Component | Pediatric (Child/Infant) Requirement |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Sequence** | **C-A-B** (Compressions, Airway, Breathing). |
| **Compression Rate** | 100 - 120 per minute. |
| **Depth (Infant)** | ~4 cm (1/3 AP diameter of chest). |
| **Depth (Child)** | ~5 cm (1/3 AP diameter of chest). |
| **Ratio (1 Rescuer)** | **30:2** (for all ages except neonates). |
| **Ratio (2 Rescuers)** | **15:2** for infants and children. |
| **Pulse Check Site** | **Infants**: Brachial; **Children**: Carotid or Femoral. (Check for 5-10 seconds). |
| **AED Pads** | Adult pads for ≥8 years; Pediatric pads preferred for <8 years (but use adult if unavailable). |

- <span style="color:red">Chest Recoil</span> must be complete between compressions to allow for atrial refilling (diastole).
- <span style="color:red">Rescue Breaths</span> should be delivered over 1 second, ensuring visible chest rise while avoiding excessive ventilation (which increases intrathoracic pressure).
- <span style="color:red">Jaw-Thrust Maneuver</span> is the mandatory technique for opening the airway in any patient with suspected cervical spine injury.
- <span style="color:red">Two Thumb-Encircling Hands Technique</span> is the preferred compression method for infants when there are two rescuers.
- <span style="color:red">Gasping</span> is not normal breathing; it is a sign of cardiac arrest and acidosis requiring immediate CPR.
- <span style="color:red">AED Analysis</span>: If the rhythm is shockable, clear the patient, deliver the shock, and **immediately resume CPR** starting with compressions (do not check pulse).
- <span style="color:red">Unwitnessed Collapse</span> in children requires the lone rescuer to perform 2 minutes (5 cycles) of CPR *before* leaving to activate EMS or get an AED.
- <span style="color:red">Witnessed Collapse</span> in children allows the lone rescuer to leave immediately to activate EMS/get an AED because the cause is likely a sudden primary cardiac arrhythmia.

### **DIFFERENTIATION AND COMPARISON FOR EXAMS**

- <span style="color:red">Etomidate vs. Ketamine</span>: Both are used for hemodynamically unstable patients, but Etomidate is avoided in sepsis (adrenal suppression) while Ketamine is preferred for asthma (bronchodilatory).
- <span style="color:red">Succinylcholine vs. Rocuronium</span>: Succinylcholine has a faster/shorter onset but dangerous side effects in hyperkalemic or burned patients; Rocuronium is the safer, non-depolarizing alternative.
- <span style="color:red">Head Tilt-Chin Lift vs. Jaw Thrust</span>: Head tilt is standard, but Jaw Thrust must be used if cervical spine injury is suspected to prevent spinal cord damage.
- <span style="color:red">Primary vs. Secondary Brain Injury</span>: Primary injury is structural/irreversible damage occurring at impact; Secondary injury is physiological (edema, ischemia) and is the target of medical management.
- <span style="color:red">1-Rescuer vs. 2-Rescuer Peds CPR</span>: The compression-to-breath ratio changes from **30:2** (1-rescuer) to **15:2** (2-rescuer) to prioritize oxygenation in children.
- <span style="color:red">Infant vs. Child Pulse Checks</span>: Check the **brachial** artery in infants (under 1 year) and the **carotid/femoral** artery in children (>1 year).
- <span style="color:red">Mannitol vs. Hypertonic Saline (HTS)</span>: HTS acts as a volume expander and is preferred in hypotension; Mannitol is an osmotic diuretic that may cause hypovolemia.
- <span style="color:red">SIADH vs. Cerebral Salt Wasting (CSW)</span>: Both cause hyponatremia after brain injury; SIADH involves fluid retention (euvolemic/hypervolemic), while CSW involves massive sodium loss in urine (hypovolemic).
- <span style="color:red">Decorticate vs. Decerebrate Posturing</span>: Both indicate brain injury; Decorticate (flexion) suggests damage above the brainstem, while Decerebrate (extension) indicates a more severe brainstem injury/herniation.
- <span style="color:red">Straight vs. Curved Blades</span>: Straight blades (Miller) physically lift the epiglottis (better for peds); Curved blades (Macintosh) are placed in the vallecula to indirectly lift the epiglottis (Standard for adults).
- <span style="color:red">Witnessed vs. Unwitnessed Peds Arrest</span>: Witnessed = Get help/AED first. Unwitnessed = Do 2 minutes of CPR first.
- <span style="color:red">Normal Ventilation vs. Hyperventilation in TBI</span>: Target normal PaCO2 (35-45) for maintenance; target low PaCO2 (25-30) ONLY as a temporary rescue for acute herniation.
- <span style="color:red">Hypoxia vs. Hyperoxia in TBI</span>: Both are harmful; hypoxia starves brain tissue, while hyperoxia produces oxygen radicals that facilitate secondary brain injury.
