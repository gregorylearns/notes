### 1. PNEUMONIA
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Pneumonia</font> is defined as inflammation of the lung parenchyma and is the leading infectious cause of death globally among children younger than 5 years.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Streptococcus pneumoniae</font> is the most common bacterial pathogen in children aged 3 weeks to 5 years.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Viruses (RSV and Rhinoviruses)</font> are the most common cause of pneumonia in young children, detectable in 40–80% of cases.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Mycoplasma pneumoniae and Chlamydophila pneumoniae</font> are the primary bacterial causes of pneumonia in children aged 5 years and older.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Tachypnea</font> is the most consistent clinical manifestation of pneumonia and is often preceded by several days of upper respiratory tract infection.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Increased work of breathing</font> in pneumonia is evidenced by intercostal, subcostal, and suprasternal retractions, nasal flaring, and in severe cases, cyanosis. 
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Bacterial pneumonia</font> typically begins suddenly with high fever, cough, chest pain, and may include delirium or abdominal pain.*
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Vira pneumonia</font> chest X-ray findings usually show hyperinflation with bilateral interstitial infiltrates and peribronchial cuffing.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Lobar consolidation</font> on a chest X-ray is typically associated with pneumococcal (bacterial) pneumonia.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Amoxicillin</font> at high doses (90 mg/kg/day) is the recommended first-line outpatient treatment for mildly ill children with suspected bacterial pneumonia.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Azithromycin</font> is the preferred choice for atypical pathogens in school-age children and adolescents with pneumonia.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Ampicillin or Penicillin G</font> is the inpatient treatment for fully immunized, non-severely ill children with pneumonia.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Oral zinc</font> supplementation (10-20 mg/day depending on age) may reduce mortality in children hospitalized with pneumonia.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Direct spread complications (Thoracic)</font> of pneumonia include pleural effusion, empyema, and pericarditis.
- (Pneumonia) <font color="red">Hematologic spread complications</font> of pneumonia include meningitis, endocarditis, and suppurative arthritis.

### 2. DENGUE FEVER & DENGUE HEMORRHAGIC FEVER (DHF)
- (Dengue) <font color="red">Dengue Virus</font> consists of 4 distinct antigenic serotypes (1, 2, 3, 4) of the Flaviviridae family, transmitted by the Aedes aegypti mosquito.
- (Dengue) <font color="red">Dengue Fever (DF)</font> is characterized by biphasic fever, myalgia, retroorbital pain ("back-break fever"), and lymphadenopathy.
- (Dengue) <font color="red">Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever (DHF)</font> is distinguished from DF by capillary permeability, abnormal hemostasis, and potential protein-losing shock syndrome.
- (Dengue) <font color="red">The Critical Phase</font> of dengue typically occurs on days 3–7 of illness, coinciding with defervescence (temperature drop), where plasma leakage and shock are most likely to occur.
- (Dengue) <font color="red">Herman’s Rash</font>, described as "isles of white in a sea of red," is a recovery phase rash associated with generalized pruritus and bradycardia.
- (Dengue) <font color="red">CLLLAMP</font> is the mnemonic for Warning Signs: Abdominal pain (C-ramps), Liver enlargement, Lethargy, Liquid (effusion), Mucosal bleed, and Platelet drop/Hct rise.
- (Dengue) <font color="red">Dengue NS1 Antigen</font> is used for early diagnosis and can become positive as early as 24 hours after symptom onset.
- (Dengue) <font color="red">Dengue IgM</font> becomes detectable at day 5 of illness and remains for 6-12 weeks, while <font color="red">Dengue IgG</font> indicates a past infection.
- (Dengue) <font color="red">The WHO DHF Criteria</font> requires fever (2-7 days), thrombocytopenia (<100,000/uL), and evidence of increased capillary permeability (Hct rise >20%).
- (Dengue) <font color="red">Aspirin</font> is strictly contraindicated in dengue patients because of its adverse effects on hemostasis and risk of Reye syndrome.
- (Dengue) <font color="red">Isotonic Crystalloids (Normal Saline)</font> are the treatment of choice for managing dengue shock syndrome; plasma/colloids are used only if Hct remains high or pulse pressure is ≤10 mmHg.
- (Dengue) <font color="red">Overhydration</font> is a major cause of fatalities during the recovery phase, leading to life-threatening hypervolemia.

### 3. TUBERCULOSIS (TB)
- (TB) <font color="red">Mycobacterium tuberculosis</font> is an obligate aerobic, acid-fast bacillus with a lipid-rich (waxy) cell wall.
- (TB) <font color="red">Presumptive TB</font> in children is defined by 3 of the following: cough/wheeze >2 wks, unexplained fever >2 wks, weight loss/FTT, or lethargy.
- (TB) <font color="red">Ghon Complex</font> is the hallmark pathological finding involving a parenchymal lung focus and involved regional lymph nodes.
- (TB) <font color="red">Xpert MTB/RIF</font> is the preferred rapid PCR test that detects TB and rifampicin resistance within 2 hours.
- (TB) <font color="red">Tuberculin Skin Test (TST)</font> is considered positive in high-risk or symptomatic children if the induration is >5 mm.
- (TB) <font color="red">Pulmonary TB Treatment</font> consists of a 2-month Intensive Phase (HRZE) and a 4-month Continuation Phase (HR).
- (TB) <font color="red">Isoniazid Preventive Therapy (IPT)</font> is mandatory for 6 months in children <5 years old who are household contacts of TB cases, regardless of symptoms.
- (TB) <font color="red">Rifampin</font> is known to turn secretions such as urine and tears into an orange color.
- (TB) <font color="red">Ethambutol</font> requires monitoring for optic neuritis, specifically affecting visual acuity and color perception.

### 4. MEASLES
- (Measles) <font color="red">Measles virus</font> is a negative-sense single-stranded RNA virus from the Paramyxoviridae family.
- (Measles) <font color="red">Koplik Spots</font> are the pathognomonic sign, appearing as red lesions with bluish-white centers on the inner cheeks 1-4 days before the rash.
- (Measles) <font color="red">The 3 Cs triad</font> of measles consists of Cough, Coryza (runny nose), and Conjunctivitis.
- (Measles) <font color="red">The Measles Rash</font> begins on the forehead/hairline and spreads downward (cephalocaudal) to the torso and extremities.
- (Measles) <font color="red">Warthin-Finkeldey giant cells</font> are multinucleated pathognomonic cells found in body tissues during the prodromal phase.
- (Measles) <font color="red">Vitamin A</font> administration (200,000 IU for children ≥12 months) is recommended for all acute measles cases to reduce morbidity.
- (Measles) <font color="red">Pneumonia</font> is the most common cause of death in measles patients.
- (Measles) <font color="red">Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE)</font> is a rare, fatal, delayed complication caused by persistent CNS infection with an altered measles virus.
- (Measles) <font color="red">Postexposure prophylaxis</font> includes giving the measles vaccine within 72 hours or Immunoglobulin within 6 days of exposure.

### 5. URINARY TRACT INFECTION (UTI)
- (UTI) <font color="red">Escherichia coli (UPEC)</font> is the most common cause of community-acquired pediatric UTIs, accounting for 80-90% of cases.
- (UTI) <font color="red">P-fimbriae</font> are hair-like appendages on E. coli that bind to Gal-Gal receptors, allowing the bacteria to resist being flushed by urine and facilitating pyelonephritis.
- (UTI) <font color="red">Pyelonephritis</font> (Upper UTI) involves the renal parenchyma and presents with high fever, vomiting, and flank/back pain.
- (UTI) <font color="red">Cystitis</font> (Lower UTI) involves the bladder, causing pain and urgency, but does NOT cause high fever or renal injury.
- (UTI) <font color="red">Nitrites</font> on a urinalysis have high specificity for UTI but low sensitivity, as they require ~4 hours for bacteria to convert.
- (UTI) <font color="red">Urine Culture</font> is the "Gold Standard" for diagnosis; ≥50,000 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen plus pyuria is diagnostic in symptomatic children.
- (UTI) <font color="red">Vesicoureteral Reflux (VUR)</font> is a condition where urine flows backward toward the kidneys, acting as a highway for bacteria.
- (UTI) <font color="red">Renal Ultrasound</font> is recommended for all infants 2–24 months after their first febrile UTI to check for structural abnormalities.
- (UTI) <font color="red">Uncircumcised males</font> have a higher incidence of UTIs during the first 3 months of life compared to females.

### 6. NEONATAL SEPSIS
- (Sepsis) <font color="red">Early-Onset Sepsis</font> occurs ≤3 days of life and is typically acquired via vertical transmission from the birth canal (GBS, E. coli).
- (Sepsis) <font color="red">Late-Onset Sepsis</font> occurs >3 days of life and is typically acquired horizontally from healthcare workers or the environment (CONS is most common).
- (Sepsis) <font color="red">Temperature instability</font> (can be fever or hypothermia) is a hallmark early sign of neonatal sepsis.
- (Sepsis) <font color="red">Prematurity</font> is the strongest risk factor for the development of neonatal sepsis.
- (Sepsis) <font color="red">Maternal chorioamnionitis (Triple I)</font> is a significant risk factor that should prompt immediate investigation of the neonate.
- (Sepsis) <font color="red">I/T Ratio</font> (Immature to Total neutrophil ratio) ≥0.2 is a sensitive biomarker for neonatal sepsis.
- (Sepsis) <font color="red">Ampicillin and Gentamicin</font> is the standard empiric antibiotic regimen for early-onset neonatal sepsis.
- (Sepsis) <font color="red">Sepsis is unlikely</font> if properly obtained blood cultures remain negative at 48 hours.

### 7. MENINGITIS
- (Meningitis) <font color="red">Streptococcus pneumoniae</font> is currently the most common cause of bacterial meningitis beyond the neonatal period in vaccinated populations.
- (Meningitis) <font color="red">Acute bacterial meningitis</font> presents in the CSF with high protein (>100 mg/dL), low glucose (<40 mg/dL), and a high WBC count (neutrophil predominance). 
- (Meningitis) <font color="red">Meningeal signs</font> such as Nuchal rigidity, Kernig, and Brudzinski signs are often unreliable in children younger than 12–18 months.
- (Meningitis) <font color="red">Lumbar Puncture (LP)</font> is the most important diagnostic step but must be delayed if there are signs of markedly increased ICP (e.g., bulging fontanelle, bradycardia, hypertension).
- (Meningitis) <font color="red">Empiric antibiotic therapy</font> (Ceftriaxone + Vancomycin) must be started immediately and should NOT be delayed for imaging or LP if the patient is unstable.
- (Meningitis) <font color="red">Dexamethasone</font> administered before or with the first antibiotic dose reduces the risk of hearing loss, especially in Hib meningitis cases.
- (Meningitis) <font color="red">Rifampin</font> is used for chemoprophylaxis of close contacts in cases of N. meningitidis or H. influenzae type b, but not for S. pneumoniae.
- (Meningitis) <font color="red">Sensorineural hearing loss</font> is a major long-term sequela; thus, hearing assessment is vital before or after discharge.

### **Comparison Points for Differentiating Confusing Topics**

1.  **Viral vs. Bacterial Pneumonia:** <font color="red">Viral pneumonia</font> usually presents with a lower fever and interstitial infiltrates on CXR, whereas <font color="red">Bacterial pneumonia</font> has a sudden onset of high fever and lobar consolidation.
2.  **Dengue Fever vs. DHF:** The primary differentiator is the presence of <font color="red">plasma leakage</font> in **DHF** (evidenced by Hct rise, pleural effusion, or low albumin), which is absent in simple **Dengue Fever**.
3.  **Dengue vs. Measles Rash:** <font color="red">Measles rash</font> is cephalocaudal (head to toe), while the <font color="red">Dengue recovery rash</font> (Herman's rash) is generalized with "isles of white," often appearing after the fever has subsided.
4.  **Cystitis vs. Pyelonephritis:** <font color="red">Cystitis</font> involves lower tract symptoms (dysuria/urgency) WITHOUT high fever or renal risk; <font color="red">Pyelonephritis</font> is an upper tract infection typically with high fever and systemic illness.
5.  **Early-Onset vs. Late-Onset Sepsis:** <font color="red">Early-onset</font> (≤3 days) is usually GBS or E. coli from the mother; <font color="red">Late-onset</font> (>3 days) is often Coagulase-negative staphylococcus (CONS) from the hospital environment.
6.  **Measles vs. Kawasaki:** <font color="red">Measles</font> presents with a severe prodromal cough and Koplik spots; <font color="red">Kawasaki</font> lacks the cough/Koplik spots and features thrombocytosis and extremity changes.
7.  **Bacterial vs. Viral Meningitis (CSF):** <font color="red">Bacterial</font> CSF shows very low glucose and neutrophil predominance; <font color="red">Viral</font> CSF shows normal/near-normal glucose and lymphocyte predominance.
8.  **Wernicke’s vs. Korsakoff’s:** <font color="red">Wernicke’s</font> is the acute, reversible triad of confusion, ataxia, and diplopia; <font color="red">Korsakoff’s</font> is the chronic, irreversible stage characterized by confabulation.
9.  **ALS vs. SMA:** <font color="red">ALS</font> involves both Upper and Lower motor neurons; <font color="red">SMA</font> is purely a Lower motor neuron disease affecting the spinal cord.
10. **ApoE ε4 vs. ApoE ε2 in AD:** <font color="red">ApoE ε4</font> increases the risk of Alzheimer's; <font color="red">ApoE ε2</font> is protective and reduces the risk.
11. **Bulbar vs. Pseudobulbar Palsy:** <font color="red">Bulbar</font> is an LMN lesion showing tongue atrophy and fasciculations; <font color="red">Pseudobulbar</font> is a UMN lesion characterized by emotional lability (involuntary laughing/crying).
12. **SMA 1 vs. SMA 3:** <font color="red">SMA 1</font> affects floppy infants and is often fatal early; <font color="red">SMA 3</font> is juvenile-onset and allows for a longer, though progressive, life.
13. **Zika vs. Dengue:** <font color="red">Zika</font> is often associated with conjunctivitis and milder fever; <font color="red">Dengue</font> has much higher fevers and more severe bone/muscle pain.
14. **Lead vs. Arsenic Poisoning:** <font color="red">Lead</font> presents with blue/gray gum lines and basophilic stippling; <font color="red">Arsenic</font> presents with Mees' lines (white streaks on nails) and scaling skin.
15. **Kernig vs. Brudzinski Sign:** <font color="red">Kernig</font> is resistance to knee extension when the hip is flexed; <font color="red">Brudzinski</font> is involuntary hip/knee flexion when the neck is flexed.

### **Differentiation & Comparison for Exams**

- **<font color="red">Biliary Atresia vs. Neonatal Hepatitis</font>**: On liver biopsy, **<font color="red">Biliary Atresia</font>** shows bile duct proliferation and portal fibrosis, while **<font color="red">Neonatal Hepatitis</font>** shows giant cell transformation and lobular disarray.
- **<font color="red">Pyloric Stenosis vs. GERD</font>**: **<font color="red">Pyloric Stenosis</font>** presents with projectile, nonbilious vomiting and a palpable olive mass; **<font color="red">GERD</font>** involves effortless regurgitation without an abdominal mass.
- **<font color="red">Hirschsprung vs. Functional Constipation</font>**: **<font color="red">Hirschsprung</font>** starts at birth (delayed meconium), lacks encopresis (fecal soiling), and has an empty rectum on exam; **<font color="red">Functional Constipation</font>** starts later, often involves encopresis, and shows a rectum full of stool.
- **<font color="red">Cushing vs. Curling Ulcers</font>**: **<font color="red">Cushing Ulcer</font>** is a stress ulcer caused by increased intracranial pressure (CNS); **<font color="red">Curling Ulcer</font>** is a stress ulcer resulting from severe systemic burns.
- **<font color="red">Primary vs. Secondary Jaundice</font>**: **<font color="red">Physiologic Jaundice</font>** appears after 24 hours (usually days 2-4); **<font color="red">Pathologic Jaundice</font>** appears within the first 24 hours of life.
- **<font color="red">Intussusception vs. Gastroenteritis</font>**: **<font color="red">Intussusception</font>** pain is paroxysmal/episodic with "calm" intervals; **<font color="red">Gastroenteritis</font>** pain and diarrhea are generally continuous.
- **<font color="red">Unconjugated vs. Conjugated Bilirubin</font>**: **<font color="red">Unconjugated</font>** is lipid-soluble and can cross the blood-brain barrier (neurotoxic); **<font color="red">Conjugated</font>** is water-soluble, excreted in urine, and not neurotoxic but implies structural/liver disease.
- **<font color="red">Meconium Ileus vs. Meconium Plug</font>**: **<font color="red">Meconium Ileus</font>** is associated with Cystic Fibrosis and involves the terminal ileum; **<font color="red">Meconium Plug Syndrome</font>** is a transient obstruction of the colon often seen in infants of diabetic mothers.
- **<font color="red">Gastric vs. Duodenal Ulcer</font>**: Pain in **<font color="red">Duodenal Ulcers</font>** is typically relieved by food ingestion, whereas **<font color="red">Gastric Ulcer</font>** pain may be exacerbated by eating.
- **<font color="red">Short-segment vs. Long-segment Hirschsprung</font>**: **<font color="red">Short-segment</font>** (rectosigmoid) is the most common (80%) and has a 4:1 male predominance; **<font color="red">Long-segment</font>** has a higher familial incidence.
