## Summary

| TOPIC | ASPECT | DETAILS |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cervical Cancer Overview** | **Global Burden** | <span style="color:red">Cervical cancer</span> is the fourth most common cancer in women globally, with approximately 660,000 new cases in 2022. |
|  | **Local Burden (PH)** | <span style="color:red">Cervical cancer</span> ranks second in cancers among women in the Philippines and second among women aged 15–44. |
|  | **Etiology** | <span style="color:red">Cervical cancer</span> is primarily caused by persistent infection with the <span style="color:red">Human Papillomavirus (HPV)</span>. |
|  | **HIV Association** | <span style="color:red">Women living with HIV</span> are 6 times more likely to develop cervical cancer compared to women without HIV. |
| **Cervical Anatomy** | **Ectocervix** | The <span style="color:red">ectocervix</span> is the portion of the cervix in the vagina and is lined by squamous epithelium. |
|  | **Endocervix** | The <span style="color:red">endocervix</span> is the cervical canal and is lined by columnar epithelium. |
|  | **Squamocolumnar Junction (SCJ)** | The <span style="color:red">Squamocolumnar Junction (SCJ)</span> is the meeting point of squamous and columnar epithelium, usually located at the ectocervix. |
|  | **Transformation Zone** | The <span style="color:red">Transformation Zone</span> is the area between the original SCJ and the current SCJ, including areas of metaplasia; it is the primary site for pre-malignancy and malignancy development. |
|  | **Nabothian Follicle** | A <span style="color:red">Nabothian follicle</span> (or cyst) occurs when squamous epithelium covers mucous columnar epithelium, leading to mucus retention. |
| **Risk Factors** | **Age & Sex** | <span style="color:red">Cervical cancer risk</span> peaks at age 20–24; risk factors are similar for premalignant lesions of the cervix, vagina, and vulva. |
|  | **Sexual History** | <span style="color:red">Risk factors for cervical cancer</span> include young age at first sexual intercourse, a greater number of sex partners, and a male partner with an uncircumcised status or risky sexual history. |
|  | **Reproductive factors** | <span style="color:red">Multiparity</span> and young age at the first full-term pregnancy are associated with increased cervical cancer risk. |
|  | **External Exposures** | <span style="color:red">Smoking cigarettes</span> and historical exposure to <span style="color:red">diethylstilbestrol (DES)</span> in the womb are identified risk factors. |
| **Clinical Presentation** | **Vaginal Bleeding** | <span style="color:red">Irregular vaginal bleeding</span>, specifically occurring after intercourse (postcoital) or douching, is a hallmark symptom. |
|  | **Discharge** | <span style="color:red">Foul-smelling vaginal discharge</span> in cervical cancer is often due to the presence of necrotic tissues. |
|  | **Advanced Symptoms** | <span style="color:red">Pelvic pain</span> and pain during sexual activity (dyspareunia) often manifest in late or middle stages of the disease. |
| **HPV Pathogenesis** | **High-Risk Strains** | <span style="color:red">HPV types 16 and 18</span> are high-risk strains responsible for >75% of cervical cancers and >50% of vaginal/vulvar cancers. |
|  | **Low-Risk Strains** | <span style="color:red">HPV types 6 and 11</span> are non-cancer causing types responsible for 90% of anogenital warts. |
|  | **Oncogenes** | The high-risk <span style="color:red">HPV E6 and E7 gene products</span> bind to <span style="color:red">p53</span>, preventing its normal activities of cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and DNA repair. |
|  | **Infection Site** | <span style="color:red">HPV</span> enters host cells in the basal layer of the epithelium, primarily at the junctional zone, via cell surface heparan sulfate. |
|  | **Progression Time** | It typically takes <span style="color:red">10–30 years</span> for an HPV infection to develop into invasive cervical cancer. |

| HISTOLOGICAL TYPE | KEY CHARACTERISTICS | ASSOCIATIONS/UNIQUE FEATURES |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)** | Most common (85–90%); features "anastomosing tongues" or cords of keratinized cells. | Strongly associated with HPV. |
| **Adenocarcinoma** | 10–15% of cases; exhibits a "barrel" configuration grossly. | Contains intracytoplasmic mucin; poorer prognosis than SCC. |
| **Clear Cell Adenocarcinoma** | Histologically identical to those of the ovary and vagina. | Associated with DES exposure; **not** associated with HPV. |
| **Verrucous Carcinoma** | Large bulbous, warty masses; resembles condyloma. | Predominantly exophytic; rarely metastasizes but virulent if mixed with typical SCC. |
| **Adenoma Malignum** | Also known as "minimal deviation adenocarcinoma." | Innocuous/bland histologic appearance but deeply invasive and virulent. |

| STAGE | EXTENT OF DISEASE |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Stage I** | Carcinoma strictly confined to the cervix (disregard extension to the corpus). |
| **Stage IA1** | Microinvasive: Invasion ≤ 3 mm in depth and ≤ 7 mm horizontal. |
| **Stage IA2** | Microinvasive: Invasion > 3 mm but ≤ 5 mm in depth and ≤ 7 mm horizontal. |
| **Stage IB1** | Invasive: > 5 mm depth and ≤ 2 cm in greatest dimension. |
| **Stage IB2** | Clinical lesions > 2 cm but ≤ 4 cm. |
| **Stage IB3** | Clinical lesions > 4 cm. |
| **Stage II** | Invades beyond uterus but NOT to the lower 1/3 of vagina or pelvic wall. |
| **Stage IIA1** | Involvement of upper 2/3 of vagina, no parametrial involvement, tumor < 4 cm. |
| **Stage IIB** | Parametrial involvement but not up to the pelvic wall. |
| **Stage III** | Involves lower 1/3 of vagina, pelvic wall, causes hydronephrosis, or involves nodes. |
| **Stage IIIC1** | Pelvic lymph node metastasis only (identified via imaging 'r' or pathology 'p'). |
| **Stage IIIC2** | Paraaortic lymph node metastasis. |
| **Stage IV** | Extended beyond true pelvis or involves mucosa of bladder/rectum (biopsy-proven). |

| MANAGEMENT STAGE | PRIMARY TREATMENT OPTIONS |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Stage 0 (CIS)** | <span style="color:red">Conization</span>, <span style="color:red">Laser surgery</span>, <span style="color:red">LEEP</span>, or <span style="color:red">Cryotherapy</span> (to preserve fertility). |
| **Stage I** | <span style="color:red">Radical hysterectomy</span> (cervix, uterus, part of vagina, nodes) or <span style="color:red">Trachelectomy</span> (fertility-sparing). |
| **Stage II and beyond** | <span style="color:red">Chemoradiation</span> (Radiation combined with low-dose chemotherapy). |

**BULLET POINTS (STAND-ALONE)**

* [Burden] At least <span style="color:red">12 Filipino women die daily</span> from cervical cancer according to May 2023 DOH data.
* [Pathogenesis] In most cases (over 80%), <span style="color:red">HPV infections</span> are transient, asymptomatic, and resolve spontaneously within 2 years.
* [Pathogenesis] Persistent infection with <span style="color:red">High-risk HPV</span> is the primary driver of cervical cancer genesis.
* [Anatomy] The <span style="color:red">Transformation Zone</span> of the cervix is the specific site where Nabothian cysts, pre-malignancy, and malignancy develop.
* [Anatomy] In prepubertal females, the <span style="color:red">SCJ</span> is located inner, while in menstruation/pregnancy, it everses and moves outer, making it more vulnerable.
* [Symptoms] <span style="color:red">Postmenopausal bleeding</span> should always be investigated for potential cervical or endometrial malignancy.
* [Diagnosis] <span style="color:red">Colposcopy and Endocervical Curettage (ECC)</span> are the required follow-up procedures for evaluating abnormal cervical cytology (Pap smear) results.
* [Diagnosis] During colposcopy, <span style="color:red">Acetic Acid (VIA)</span> is applied to highlight "acetowhite" areas that indicate abnormal epithelium for biopsy.
* [Histology] <span style="color:red">Squamous cell carcinoma</span> is the most frequent histologic type, and the large cell, non-keratinizing type is most common.
* [Histology] <span style="color:red">Adenocarcinoma</span> of the cervix often presents with a characteristic **"barrel" configuration** grossly. 
* [Histology] <span style="color:red">Adenoma malignum</span>, while appearing histologically bland, is a highly virulent variety of adenocarcinoma that metastasizes early.
* [Histology] <span style="color:red">Clear cell adenocarcinoma</span> is unique because it is not associated with the HPV virus but is linked to DES exposure.
* [Grading] <span style="color:red">Grade 1 SCC</span> is well-differentiated and shows keratin pearls; <span style="color:red">Grade 3 SCC</span> is poorly differentiated with abundant necrosis.
* [Prognosis] The <span style="color:red">FIGO stage</span> is the most important determinant of prognosis for carcinoma of the cervix.
* [Prognosis] <span style="color:red">Tumor size</span> is one of the most important predictors for local recurrence of cervical cancer.
* [Prognosis] <span style="color:red">Surgical sampling of lymph nodes</span> is the most sensitive method for evaluating regional metastasis, outperforming CT or MRI.
* [Staging] For <span style="color:red">FIGO Staging</span>, if there is doubt between two stages, the lower (less advanced) stage should be assigned.
* [Staging] <span style="color:red">Stage IA</span> cervical cancer is diagnosed by microscopy only; once it is clinically visible, it is staged as IB or higher.
* [Management] <span style="color:red">Trachelectomy</span> is a surgical option for Stage 1 patients who are young and wish to preserve their fertility.
* [Management] <span style="color:red">Radical hysterectomy</span> differs from basic hysterectomy as it removes the cervix, upper vagina, and parametrium, and includes a bilateral node dissection.
* [Management] For <span style="color:red">Stage II and beyond</span>, chemoradiation (radiation plus chemotherapy) is the preferred treatment approach over surgery.
* [Prevention] <span style="color:red">Total abstinence</span> from genital contact is the most effective method for preventing HPV.
* [Vaccination] The <span style="color:red">Quadrivalent HPV vaccine</span> can be administered to females (9-45 years) and males (9-26 years).
* [Vaccination] The <span style="color:red">Bivalent HPV vaccine</span> is approved for girls and women from 9 years of age onwards.
* [Vaccination] For children <span style="color:red">under 15 years old</span>, the CDC recommends only 2 doses of the HPV vaccine (0 and 6-12 month schedule).
* [Vaccination] For individuals <span style="color:red">15 years or older</span> or those who are immunocompromised, 3 doses of the HPV vaccine are required.
* [Vaccination] <span style="color:red">HPV Vaccination</span> is most effective if given before "sexual debut" (first sexual encounter).
* [Screening] An <span style="color:red">HPV vaccine</span> does **not** replace the need for regular Pap smear screening.
* [Screening] If both <span style="color:red">HPV test and Pap smear</span> are negative, screening can be spaced out to every 2 years in low-risk individuals.

**DIFFERENTIATION AND COMPARISONS (EXAM HIGH-YIELD)**

1. [Viral Strain] <span style="color:red">HPV 16 & 18</span> are oncogenic (cancer), whereas <span style="color:red">HPV 6 & 11</span> are non-oncogenic (genital warts).
2. [Morphology] <span style="color:red">Squamous Cell Carcinoma</span> typically presents as a cauliflower-like mass, whereas <span style="color:red">Adenocarcinoma</span> often presents with a "barrel-type" configuration.
3. [Pathogenesis] <span style="color:red">HPV E6</span> protein targets p53 degradation, whereas <span style="color:red">HPV E7</span> protein interacts with the Retinoblastoma (Rb) protein (implied in cell cycle regulation).
4. [Histology Association] <span style="color:red">SCC</span> is almost always HPV-related; <span style="color:red">Clear Cell Adenocarcinoma</span> is specifically associated with DES exposure and is non-HPV-related.
5. [Gross Appearance] <span style="color:red">Exophytic masses</span> grow outward (easier to detect), while <span style="color:red">Endophytic masses</span> grow inward (frequently harder to detect early).
6. [Staging] <span style="color:red">Stage IA</span> is microscopic only; <span style="color:red">Stage IB</span> represents clinically visible lesions or microscopic invasion deeper than 5mm.
7. [Microinvasive Cutoff] <span style="color:red">Stage IA1</span> invasion is ≤ 3 mm; <span style="color:red">Stage IA2</span> invasion is > 3 mm but ≤ 5 mm.
8. [Vaginal Involvement] <span style="color:red">Stage IIA</span> involves the upper 2/3 of the vagina; <span style="color:red">Stage IIIA</span> involves the lower 1/3 of the vagina.
9. [Parametrial Involvement] <span style="color:red">Stage IIB</span> involves the parametrium but not the pelvic wall; <span style="color:red">Stage IIIB</span> extends to the pelvic wall or causes hydronephrosis.
10. [Metastasis Staging] <span style="color:red">Stage IIIC1</span> involves pelvic lymph nodes only; <span style="color:red">Stage IIIC2</span> involves paraaortic lymph nodes.
11. [Radiation vs Surgery] <span style="color:red">Stage I</span> is primarily managed with surgery; <span style="color:red">Stage II, III, and IV</span> are primarily managed with chemoradiation.
12. [Hysterectomy Type] <span style="color:red">Basic Hysterectomy</span> removes just the uterus/cervix; <span style="color:red">Radical Hysterectomy</span> includes the parametrium, upper vagina, and pelvic lymphadenectomy.
13. [Fertility Preservation] <span style="color:red">Conization</span> is used for Stage 0 (CIS), while <span style="color:red">Trachelectomy</span> is used for Stage 1 invasive cancer to preserve the uterus.
14. [Warty Lesions] <span style="color:red">Condyloma acuminata</span> (benign warts) have a fibroelastic basement, while <span style="color:red">Verrucous carcinoma</span> (malignant) lacks it.
15. [Survival Rate] <span style="color:red">Stage 1</span> has an 86% 5-year survival rate, while <span style="color:red">Stage 4</span> drops significantly to 7%.
16. [Screening Frequency] <span style="color:red">High-risk patients</span> require screening every 12 months, while <span style="color:red">Negative co-testing patients</span> may screen every 2 years.
17. [Vaccine Dosing] <span style="color:red">Age &lt15</span> requires 2 doses; <span style="color:red">Age ≥15</span> requires 3 doses.
18. [Diagnostic Tissue] <span style="color:red">Pap smear</span> is cytologic (cells); <span style="color:red">Colposcopy biopsy</span> is histologic (tissue architecture) and provides the definitive diagnosis.
19. [Adenocarcinoma Types] <span style="color:red">Usual type Adenocarcinoma</span> contains mucin and is related to HPV; <span style="color:red">Adenoma Malignum</span> is a minimal-deviation type that is highly aggressive despite appearing benign.
20. [SCC Grading] <span style="color:red">Grade 2 (Moderately differentiated)</span> is the most frequent incidence, occurring more often than Grade 1 or Grade 3.
21. [Diagnostic Tools] <span style="color:red">IMAGING (MRI/CT)</span> is useful for node identification but <span style="color:red">SURGERY</span> remains the most sensitive method for nodal staging.

### I. THE MENSTRUAL CYCLE AND ENDOMETRIAL ANATOMY

| TOPIC | PATHOGENESIS / PHYSIOLOGY | CLINICAL CORRELATION |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Menstrual Cycle Goal** | Ensure an appropriate environment for implantation of the developing conceptus. | Suspected Endometrial CA: Always ask about the previous 3 menstrual cycles. |
| **Hormonal Shifts** | Menstruation is triggered by the decrease in <font color="red">estrogen and progesterone</font>. | Progesterone antagonizes estradiol's mitotic action by decreasing receptors and converting estradiol to estrone. |
| **Stratum Basale** | <font color="red">Stratum basale</font> is the layer on top of the myometrium containing primordial glands and dense stroma; it does NOT desquamate. | This layer remains stable and changes little during the menstrual cycle. |
| **Stratum Functionale** | <font color="red">Stratum functionale</font> is the layer of the endometrium between the basale and the lumen; it consists of the stratum compactum (superficial) and stratum spongiosum (lower). | This is the layer mainly affected by hormones during the menstrual cycle and is shed during menstruation. |
| **Follicular Phase** | <font color="red">DNA synthesis and mitoses</font> increase in the follicular phase as the endometrium thickens from 1-2 mm to ~12 mm at ovulation. | Straight glands become progressively more voluminous and tortuous towards the late follicular phase. |
| **Luteal Phase** | <font color="red">Luteal phase endometrium</font> undergoes secretory differentiation, characterized by subnuclear glycogen-rich vacuoles. | Implantation window occurs typically on days 20 to 24 of a 28-day cycle. |
| **Menstruation** | <font color="red">Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)</font> are proteolytic enzymes that cause intense tissue breakdown and bleeding when hCG is absent. | Average blood loss is 35 mL (Normal: 10-80 mL) over 3 to 5 days. |
| **Plasmin** | <font color="red">Plasmin</font> is the specific enzyme that inhibits blood clotting during menstruation. | This explains why menstrual blood typically does not clot. |

*   In the <font color="red">follicular phase</font> of the cycle, sonography shows endometrial thickness increasing from 4 mm in the early phase to 12 mm at ovulation.
*   In the <font color="red">luteal phase</font> of the cycle, sonography shows the endometrial thickness remains stable between 8 mm and 14 mm.
*   <font color="red">Glycodelin</font> is a protein produced during the secretory phase that plays a key role in creating a suitable uterine environment for pregnancy and regulating fertilization.
*   <font color="red">Decidualization</font> is the process that follows predecidualization of the stroma specifically if pregnancy occurs.

### II. UTERINE CANCER EPIDEMIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS

| TOPIC | KEY CHARACTERISTICS | RISK IMPLICATIONS |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Endometrial Adenocarcinoma** | Primarily affects perimenopausal and postmenopausal women (Ages 50-65). | Lifetime risk of diagnosis is approximately 3.1%. |
| **Obesity** | The strongest risk factor for <font color="red">endometrial cancer</font> due to increased circulating estrogen. | BMI >30 increases risk two- to threefold. |
| **Lynch Syndrome** | An inherited DNA repair disorder (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) causing 5% of cases. | Patients have a 40-60% lifetime risk of both endometrial and colon cancer. |
| **Tamoxifen** | A drug used for breast cancer that increases the risk of <font color="red">endometrial cancer</font> due to its estrogenic effect on the uterus. | Cases are mostly endometrioid and low grade, but high-grade sarcomas are reported. |

*   <font color="red">Unchecked Estrogen</font> (endogenous or exogenous) is the primary driver of endometrial proliferation, hyperplasia, and cancer.
*   <font color="red">Diabetes</font> is an independent risk factor for endometrial cancer, carrying a 2.8x increased risk.
*   <font color="red">Nulliparity</font> is a reproductive factor associated with a 2x increased risk of endometrial cancer.
*   The <font color="red">most important risk factors</font> for endometrial cancer include:
    1. Endometrial hyperplasia
    2. Reproductive factors (Early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity)
    3. PCOS (Stein-Leventhal syndrome)
    4. Postmenopausal estrogen therapy
    5. Obesity
    6. Tamoxifen use
    7. Lynch Syndrome
*   <font color="red">Decreased risk</font> of endometrial cancer is associated with:
    1. Pregnancy and lactation (breaks the cycle of unopposed estrogen)
    2. Combination oral contraceptives (starts after 1 year of use; lasts 15 years after stopping).
    3. Physical activity
    4. Smoking (due to weight gain/estrogen effects, though medically harmful otherwise).

### V. ENDOMETRIAL CANCER (TYPES, STAGING, AND MANAGEMENT)

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Endometrioid** | Inner lining / Type I. | 75-80% of cases; usually early stage; favorable prognosis. |
| **Serous** | Columnar epithelium / Type II. | Highly aggressive; poor prognosis. |
| **Clear Cell** | Cuboidal epithelium / Type II. | Aggressive; poor prognosis. |

*   <font color="red">Stage I Endometrial Cancer</font> is defined as cancer confined to the corpus uteri.
*   The <font color="red">Primary Management</font> for endometrial cancer is extrafascial hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (BSO), and pelvic/para-aortic lymphadenectomy.
*   <font color="red">Histologic Grade</font> is a major determinant of prognosis; Grade 1 (well-differentiated) has the best outcome.

*   <font color="red">Brachytherapy</font> involves placing radiation directly into the vagina/uterus, delivering high doses to the tumor while sparing the bladder and rectum.
*   <font color="red">Mismatch Repair Deficiency (dMMR)</font> or High Microsatellite Instability (MSI-H) makes tumors particularly vulnerable to immunotherapy.
*   <font color="red">Pembrolizumab (Keytruda)</font> is an immune checkpoint inhibitor approved for dMMR or MSI-H advanced endometrial cancer.
*   <font color="red">Lynch Syndrome</font> screening is recommended for ALL women diagnosed with endometrial cancer to help with family screening and management.

| TYPE | TISSUE OF ORIGIN | KEY FEATURES |

| **Leiomyosarcoma** | Myometrial Muscle. | 30% of sarcomas; peak age 50; NOT from benign fibroids. |

*   <font color="red">Embryonal rhabdomyosarcomas</font> (botryoides) are rare forms of uterine sarcoma found almost exclusively in infants.

### VII. COMPETENCY COMPARISONS FOR BOARD EXAMS

*   <font color="red">Endometrial vs. Cervical Cancer spread</font>: Endometrial cancer usually spreads downwards to the cervix, then sidewards, then upwards; Cervical cancer spread is characterized by lateral spread first, then upwards.
*   <font color="red">Atrophy vs. Cancer in PMB</font>: Endometrial/Vaginal atrophy is the **most common** cause of postmenopausal bleeding; however, Endometrial Cancer is the **most significant** cause that must be ruled out via biopsy.
*   <font color="red">Type I vs. Type II Endometrial Cancer</font>: Type I (Endometrioid) is estrogen-dependent, occurs in younger/perimenopausal women, and has a good prognosis; Type II (Serous/Clear cell) is estrogen-independent, occurs in older women, and is highly aggressive.
*   <font color="red">Pipelle vs. D&C</font>: Both are comparable for diagnosing general endometrial cancer, but D&C is preferred if office sampling is inadequate or if complex atypical hyperplasia is suspected as D&C has better sensitivity for finding occult cancer.
*   <font color="red">Homologous vs. Heterologous Sarcoma</font>: Homologous elements in carcinosarcoma are tissues native to the uterus (mesenchyme); Heterologous elements are tissues foreign to the uterus (bone, cartilage, striated muscle).
*   <font color="red">Hyperplasia without Atypia vs. EIN</font>: Hyperplasia without atypia is benign glandular overgrowth requiring progestins; EIN (Atypical) is a premalignant lesion where hysterectomy is the standard of care.
*   <font color="red">Stratum Basale vs. Stratum Functionale</font>: The Basale is the regenerative layer that never sheds; the Functionale is the hormone-responsive layer that desquamates during menstruation.
*   <font color="red">TVUS Cutoff (Postmenopausal vs. Premenopausal)</font>: A 4 mm cutoff is used in postmenopausal women to exclude pathology; the endometrial stripe measurement is clinically **not useful** in premenopausal women due to normal cyclic variations.
*   <font color="red">Neoadjuvant vs. Adjuvant Therapy</font>: Neoadjuvant therapy is given **before** the primary surgery (e.g., to shrink a tumor); Adjuvant therapy is given **after** surgery (e.g., to kill remaining microscopic cells).
*   <font color="red">Brachytherapy vs. External Beam Radiation (EBRT)</font>: Brachytherapy is "internal" and localized (vaginal to uterine); EBRT is "external" and travels through the abdominal wall and pelvic organs to reach the target.
*   <font color="red">Leiomyoma vs. Leiomyosarcoma</font>: Leiomyosarcoma is **not** a transformed fibroid; it is a de novo malignancy. Differential is based on mitotic count (>5 per 10 HPF) and cytologic atypia.
*   <font color="red">Low-grade vs. High-gradeESS</font>: Low-grade Endometrial Stromal Sarcoma is slow-growing and hormone-responsive; High-grade ESS is aggressive and behaves similarly to high-grade sarcomas.
*   <font color="red">Progesterone's role in Cancer vs. Hyperplasia</font>: In hyperplasia, progesterone is used as a primary treatment to reverse the overgrowth; in cancer, "high dose progestins" are used primarily for fertility preservation or patients who cannot tolerate surgery/radiation.
*   <font color="red">Lynch Syndrome MLH1/MSH2 vs. MSH6</font>: MLH1/MSH2 carriers have balanced high risks for both colon and endometrial cancer; MSH6 carriers have a higher risk for endometrial cancer with a later age of onset.
*   <font color="red">Cribriforming vs. Straight Glands</font>: Cribriforming (back-to-back glands with no intervening stroma) is pathognomonic for atypical hyperplasia; Straight/Tortuous glands are normal features of the follicular/proliferative phase.

### **GENERAL EPIDEMIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS**

* [Epidemiology] <font color="red">Ovarian Cancer</font> is the **2nd most common malignancy** of the female lower genital tract and the **most frequent cause of death** from gynecologic neoplasms.
* [Epidemiology] The incidence of <font color="red">Epithelial Ovarian Cancer (EOC)</font> increases with age, becoming most marked beyond 50 years and peaking between 51-70 years old.
* [Definitions] <font color="red">Epithelial Ovarian Cancer (EOC)</font> is considered a single entity consisting of shared clinical behavior between epithelial ovarian, fallopian, and peritoneal carcinomas.
* [Risk Factors] A <font color="red">First-degree relative</font> (mother, sister, daughter) with the disease constitutes a significant risk factor for familial ovarian cancer.
* [Risk Factors] High-risk mutations in <font color="red">BRCA1, BRCA2, and Lynch mutations</font> significantly increase the lifetime risk of developing ovarian cancer.
* [Risk Factors] The <font color="red">Lifetime Risk for Ovarian Cancer</font> is approximately 40% for BRCA1 carriers and 10-20% for BRCA2 carriers.
* [Pathogenesis] Prolonged or <font color="red">Incessant Ovulation</font> due to early age menarche or late menopause increases risk by damaging the ovary and increasing repair-related mutations.
* [Risk Factors] Conditions like <font color="red">Endometriosis</font> are specifically associated with an increased risk of Endometrioid and Clear Cell carcinomas.
* [Risk Factors] <font color="red">Fertility Drugs</font> are noted to potentially increase the risk of Borderline serous tumors.
* [Protective Factors] <font color="red">Oral Contraceptive Use</font> reduces the frequency of ovulation and thus provides protection against ovarian cancer.
* [Protective Factors] <font color="red">Lactation/Breastfeeding</font> confers protection, particularly when continued for **>18 months**, due to lactational amenorrhea.
* [Prevention] <font color="red">Opportunistic Bilateral Salpingo-oophorectomy (OBS)</font> is the removal of fallopian tubes for primary prevention during pelvic surgery for other indications.
* [Pathogenesis] The <font color="red">FSH and LH Hormones</font> may have stimulatory effects that promote growth, cell division, and mutations leading to EOC.

### **SURFACE EPITHELIAL TUMORS**

* [Epithelial Tumors] <font color="red">Serous Tumors</font> are the most common ovarian epithelial tumors and feature cells similar to the fallopian tube (ciliated, secretory, intercalated).
* [Pathogenesis] High-grade <font color="red">Serous Tumors</font> are often associated with the **P53 signature** and may arise from the fimbriated end of the fallopian tube.
* [Epithelial Tumors] <font color="red">Mucinous Tumors</font> are characterized by cells filled with abundant basophilic mucin and have the potential to grow into very large masses.
* [Pathogenesis] <font color="red">Mucinous Tumors</font> are frequently associated with **KRAS mutations** (30-40%) and may resemble goblet cells from the GI tract.
* [Epithelial Tumors] <font color="red">Endometrioid Tumors</font> consist of epithelial cells resembling the endometrium and are often seen in conjunction with endometriosis.
* [Epithelial Tumors] <font color="red">Clear Cell Tumors</font> are highly aggressive, early-stage tumors containing glycogen-rich cells and characteristic **hobnail cells**.
* [Epithelial Tumors] <font color="red">Clear Cell Tumors</font> are associated with **ARID1A mutations** and have been historically linked to intrauterine diethylstilbestrol (DES) exposure.
* [Epithelial Tumors] <font color="red">Brenner Tumors</font> are rare (2-3%) and consist of transitional-type epithelium resembling the bladder (urothelium) with a **coffee bean appearance**.
* [Pathology] <font color="red">Borderline Tumors</font> are defined by features like epithelial pleomorphism and stratification but characteristically show **no stromal invasion**.

### **GERM CELL TUMORS**

* [Germ Cell Tumors] <font color="red">Dysgerminoma</font> is the most common malignant germ cell tumor, occurring primarily in females under 30 years old, and is analogous to the male seminoma.
* [Germ Cell Tumors] <font color="red">Dysgerminoma</font> is the only malignant germ cell tumor that is frequently **bilateral** (~10% of cases).
* [Germ Cell Tumors] <font color="red">Endodermal Sinus Tumor (Yolk Sac Tumor)</font> is a rapidly growing malignancy characterized histologically by **Schiller-Duval bodies**.
* [Germ Cell Tumors] <font color="red">Mature Cystic Teratoma (Dermoid Cyst)</font> is the **most common germ cell tumor** overall and contains tissues from all three embryonic layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm).
* [Germ Cell Tumors] <font color="red">Struma Ovarii</font> is a specialized teratoma consisting of functional thyroid tissue that can occasionally cause a thyroid storm.
* [Germ Cell Tumors] <font color="red">Immature Teratomas</font> are malignant and contain **immature neuroepithelium** often organized in rosettes; prognosis depends on the histologic grade.
* [Germ Cell Tumors] <font color="red">Choriocarcinoma</font> is a rare, highly malignant tumor that produces **hCG** and consists of cytotrophoblasts and syncytiotrophoblasts.

### **SEX CORD-STROMAL TUMORS**

* [Sex Cord-Stromal] <font color="red">Granulosa Cell Tumor</font> is the most common **feminizing tumor**, often secreting estrogen and causing endometrial hyperplasia or postmenopausal bleeding.
* [Sex Cord-Stromal] <font color="red">Granulosa Cell Tumor</font> is characterized by **Call-Exner bodies** (rosette-like arrangements) and the **FOXL2 mutation**.
* [Sex Cord-Stromal] <font color="red">Sertoli-Leydig Cell Tumor</font> is a rare, **virilizing tumor** that causes defeminization (breast atrophy) and masculinization (hirsutism, deep voice) due to androgen secretion.
* [Sex Cord-Stromal] <font color="red">Fibroma</font> is the **most common benign solid** ovarian tumor and is not hormone-secreting.
* [Syndrome] <font color="red">Meigs Syndrome</font> is the classic triad of a **Benign Ovarian Fibroma, Ascites, and Pleural Effusion** that resolves after tumor removal.

### **DIAGNOSIS, STAGING, AND SURGERY**

* [Imaging] <font color="red">Ultrasonography</font> is the primary tool to confirm benign vs. malignant lesions, using criteria like IOTA or the Adnex Model.
* [Diagnostic Criteria] A <font color="red">Malignant Ultrasound Finding</font> includes an irregular solid tumor, **ascites**, at least 4 papillary structures, and very strong blood flow.
* [Diagnostic Criteria] A <font color="red">Benign Ultrasound Finding</font> includes a unilocular cyst, **acoustic shadows**, and no solid components (or components <7 mm).
* [Markers] <font color="red">CA125</font> is the most commonly used tumor marker for epithelial EOC, though its sensitivity is lower in premenopausal women.
* [Algorithm] The <font color="red">ROMA (Risk of Malignancy Algorithm)</font> utilizes menopausal status, Serum HE4, and CA125 to determine high vs. low risk of malignancy.
* [Surgery] The primary objective and gold standard for <font color="red">Ovarian Cancer Surgery</font> is **optimal tumor debulking** (complete resection with zero residual).
* [Surgery] <font color="red">Frozen Section</font> is 92.9% accurate and used intraoperatively to diagnose malignancy or site of origin to guide surgical extent.
* [Surgery Indications] An <font color="red">Ovarian Cyst >5cm</font> that does not regress within 6-8 weeks is an indication for surgical intervention.
* [Prognosis] The **most important prognostic factor** for ovarian carcinoma is the **amount of residual tumor** remaining after surgical resection.
* [Staging] <font color="red">FIGO Stage 1C1</font> refers to a surgical spill of the tumor, which is considered iatrogenic or the "surgeon's fault."
* [Treatment] <font color="red">Paclitaxel and Carboplatin</font> remains the standard chemotherapy regimen for most Epithelial Ovarian Cancers.

### **FALLOPIAN TUBE AND PERITONEAL CANCER**

* [Symptoms] <font color="red">Hydrops Tubae Profluens</font> is the intermittent discharge of serosanguinous fluid into the vagina, characteristic of fallopian tube cancer.
* [Signs] The <font color="red">Latzko Triad</font> for fallopian tube cancer consists of **colicky pain, an adnexal mass, and serosanguinous vaginal discharge**.
* [Definitions] <font color="red">Peritoneal Cancer</font> is a diagnosis of exclusion where both ovaries are normal in size, but extraovarian serous tumor involvement is significant.
* [EOC Treatment] The treatment for <font color="red">Fallopian Tube and Peritoneal Cancers</font> is identical to that of epithelial ovarian cancer.

### **METASTATIC DISEASE**

* [Metastasis] <font color="red">Krukenberg Tumors</font> are metastatic tumors to the ovary, characteristically containing **signet-ring cells** and most commonly originating from the **stomach**.

### **DIFFERENTIATING CONFUSING CLINICAL ENTITIES**

* <font color="red">Brenner Tumor vs. Granulosa Cell Tumor</font>: Brenner tumors feature "coffee bean" nuclei in transitional urothelium but are generally non-functional, whereas Granulosa Cell tumors have Call-Exner bodies and are estrogen-secreting (feminizing).
* <font color="red">Dysgerminoma vs. Yolk Sac Tumor</font>: Dysgerminoma occurs in young women and is marked by **LDH**, while Yolk Sac tumors occur in even younger populations (children/teens) and are marked by **AFP**.
* <font color="red">Call-Exner Bodies vs. Schiller-Duval Bodies</font>: **Call-Exner bodies** (rosettes with eosinophilic fluid) are found in Granulosa Cell tumors; **Schiller-Duval bodies** (perivascular formations) are found in Yolk Sac tumors.
* <font color="red">Feminizing vs. Virilizing Tumors</font>: **Granulosa Cell tumors** increase estrogen (bleeding/hyperplasia); **Sertoli-Leydig tumors** increase androgens (hirsutism/deep voice).
* <font color="red">Mature vs. Immature Teratoma</font>: **Mature** (Dermoid) is benign and contains adult tissues like hair/teeth; **Immature** is malignant and contains embryonic neuroepithelium.
* <font color="red">EOC vs. Borderline Tumors</font>: **Epithelial Ovarian Cancer** shows destructive stromal invasion and poor prognosis; **Borderline tumors** show epithelial atypia/proliferation but strictly **no stromal invasion**.
* <font color="red">CA125 vs. HE4</font>: CA125 is the standard marker but can be elevated in benign conditions (endometriosis/PID); HE4 is more specific for malignancy and less likely to be elevated in benign disease.
* <font color="red">Type 1 vs. Type 2 Serous Carcinoma</font>: Type 1 is low-grade, arises from borderline tumors, and lacks p53; Type 2 is high-grade, arises from STIC in the tube, and always has **p53 mutations**.
* <font color="red">Endometrioid vs. Clear Cell Carcinoma</font>: Both are associated with endometriosis, but Clear Cell is more aggressive and characterized by **hobnail cells** and glycogen.
* <font color="red">Primary Ovarian vs. Peritoneal Cancer</font>: In **Peritoneal cancer**, the ovaries must be normal or only minimally involved (< 5x5 mm), with bulkier tumor found on the peritoneal surfaces.
* <font color="red">Dysgerminoma vs. Choriocarcinoma</font>: Dysgerminoma is the most common malignant germ cell tumor and markers include LDH; Choriocarcinoma is rarer and produces extremely high levels of **hCG**.
* <font color="red">Krukenberg vs. Primary Ovarian Cancer</font>: **Krukenberg** is typically bilateral, solid, and contains signet-ring cells from a GI source; Primary ovarian epithelial tumors are more often cystic or mixed and arise locally.
* <font color="red">Fibroma vs. Thecoma</font>: Both are sex cord-stromal tumors, but **Fibromas** are hormone-inactive and associated with Meigs Syndrome, while Thecomas may produce estrogen.
* <font color="red">Mucinous vs. Serous Tumors</font>: **Serous** is the most common and often bilateral; **Mucinous** can become much larger, is usually unilateral, and is associated with smoking and KRAS mutations.
* <font color="red">Optimal vs. Suboptimal Debulking</font>: **Optimal** debulking traditionally meant <1 cm residual, but current goals are **complete resection** (zero gross residual) for best survival (106 months median OS).

## Summary


### TOPIC: OVERVIEW AND CLASSIFICATION OF GESTATIONAL TROPHOBLASTIC DISEASE (GTD)

| Category | Entities | Key Shared Features | Unique Features |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Benign Trophoblastic Lesions** | Exaggerated placental site, Placental site nodule | Benign proliferation | Non-neoplastic; limited invasive potential. |
| **Hydatidiform Mole (HM)** | Complete HM (CHM), Partial HM (PHM) | Premalignant; abnormal villous proliferation | Potential for invasion into the myometrium. |
| **Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN)** | Invasive mole, Choriocarcinoma, PSTT, ETT | Malignant; biochemical evidence (β-HCG) | Varying degrees of chemoresistance and metastatic potential. |

* In the context of gynecologic oncology, <font color="red">Gestational Trophoblastic Disease (GTD)</font> is considered one of the most curable gynecologic malignancies.
* Regarding the disease spectrum, <font color="red">Gestational Trophoblastic Disease (GTD)</font> ranges from benign to premalignant and malignant, characterized by abnormal trophoblastic proliferation.
* Per the WHO classification, <font color="red">Benign trophoblastic lesions</font> include the exaggerated placental site and the placental site nodule.
* Within the WHO classification, <font color="red">Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN)</font> includes invasive mole, choriocarcinoma, placental site trophoblastic tumor (PSTT), and epithelioid trophoblastic tumor (ETT).

### TOPIC: RISK FACTORS AND GENETICS OF HYDATIDIFORM MOLE (HM)

| Risk Factor Category | High-Yield Details |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Maternal Age** | Extremes of age (<15 and >45 years); 5-10x increase after age 40. |
| **Obstetric History** | Prior HM (5–40x risk); infertility; ≥2 miscarriages (1.9–3.2x risk). |
| **Dietary Intake** | Low animal fat and beta-carotene; Vitamin A deficiency. |
| **Genetics** | NLRP7/KHDC3L mutations in familial cases; disrupted genomic imprinting. |

* In the epidemiology of HM, <font color="red">Extremes of maternal age</font> (younger than 15 and older than 45 years) are a well-established risk factor for molar pregnancy.
* Regarding age-related risk, <font color="red">Advanced maternal age</font> (specifically conceiving after age 40) is associated with a 5- to 10-fold increase in HM risk due to abnormal gametogenesis.
* For patients with a history of molar pregnancy, a <font color="red">Previous HM</font> increases the risk of future HM by 5 to 40 times compared to the general population.
* In terms of recurrence, the <font color="red">Risk of subsequent molar pregnancy</font> rises to 11–25% if a patient has had two or more previous HMs.
* Regarding nutrition, a <font color="red">Low intake of animal fat and beta-carotene</font> is linked to a higher risk of Complete Hydatidiform Mole (CHM).
* In the context of dietary regionality, <font color="red">Vitamin A deficiency</font> is more common in regions with a high incidence of Gestational Trophoblastic Disease.
* For rare familial cases, <font color="red">Familial recurrent HM</font> is an autosomal recessive condition linked to chromosome 19q13.3-13.4.
* In the genetics of recurrent HMs, <font color="red">NLRP7 mutations</font> are the most common (found in ~60%) and lead to diploid biparental complete moles.
* Regarding the biologic mechanism of molar pregnancy, <font color="red">Disrupted genomic imprinting</font> (maternal gene silencing with paternal gene expression) is the key driver of development.

### TOPIC: DIFFERENTIATING COMPLETE VS. PARTIAL HYDATIDIFORM MOLE

| Feature | <font color="red">Complete Hydatidiform Mole (CHM)</font> | <font color="red">Partial Hydatidiform Mole (PHM)</font> |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Karyotype** | 46,XX (90%) or 46,XY; Paternal origin | Triploid (69,XXX, 69,XXY, 69,XYY); Maternal + Paternal |
| **Fetal Tissue** | Absent | Present (often with anomalies) |
| **Villi Appearance** | Generalized hydropic (edematous) villi; Cisterns | Focal hydropic swelling; Scalloping |
| **Trophoblast** | Diffuse hyperplasia; Marked atypia | Focal hyperplasia; Less atypia; Stromal inclusions |
| **p57 Immunostain** | **ABSENT** (Paternal origin) | **PRESENT** (Maternal contribution) |
| **Clinical Signs** | Common: Vaginal bleeding, theca lutein cysts, medical complications | Rare medical complications; mimics missed abortion |
| **β-HCG Levels** | Frequently >100,000 mIU/mL | Less markedly elevated |

* In the histopathology of <font color="red">Complete Hydatidiform Mole (CHM)</font>, the hallmark is the absence of fetal or embryonic tissues and the presence of diffuse trophoblastic hyperplasia.
* Regarding genomic origin, <font color="red">Complete Hydatidiform Mole (CHM)</font> is typically 46,XX and of purely paternal origin, resulting from the fertilization of an "empty" ovum.
* In the histopathology of <font color="red">Partial Hydatidiform Mole (PHM)</font>, the characteristics include focal hydropic swelling of villi and the presence of fetal or embryonic tissues.
* Regarding genomic origin, <font color="red">Partial Hydatidiform Mole (PHM)</font> is triploid (usually 69,XXX or 69,XXY) carrying both maternal and paternal chromosomes.
* For diagnostic immunohistochemistry, <font color="red">p57 immunostaining</font> is absent in Complete Hydatidiform Mole (CHM) because it is a maternally expressed gene.
* In terms of microscopic findings, <font color="red">Trophoblastic scalloping and stromal inclusions</font> are specific histological features seen in Partial Hydatidiform Mole (PHM).
* Regarding clinical presentation, <font color="red">Complete Hydatidiform Mole (CHM)</font> presents with a classic "snowstorm appearance" on ultrasound and markedly high β-HCG levels.

### TOPIC: DIAGNOSIS AND CLINICAL FEATURES OF HYDATIDIFORM MOLE

* In modern obstetric practice, <font color="red">Early ultrasonography and sensitive β-HCG assays</font> have led to earlier diagnosis of HM, reducing the frequency of advanced, dramatic presentations.
* Regarding symptoms of CHM, <font color="red">Vaginal bleeding</font> in the first trimester is a typical sign, often accompanied by the passage of molar vesicles.
* In the physical exam of CHM, <font color="red">Large-for-date uterus</font> and the absence of fetal movement are common findings.
* Regarding medical complications of large moles, <font color="red">Gestational hypertension before 20 weeks</font> is a classic red flag for Gestational Trophoblastic Disease.
* In the pathophysiology of molar complications, <font color="red">β-HCG homology to TSH and LH</font> can lead to hyperthyroidism and the development of ovarian theca lutein cysts, respectively.
* Regarding ovarian findings, <font color="red">Theca lutein cysts</font> are associated with high β-HCG levels and are common in CHM but rare in PHM.
* For diagnostic imaging, <font color="red">Ultrasound (UTZ)</font> is the standard modality; however, it is more accurate for diagnosing CHM (79%) than PHM (29%).
* On ultrasound, <font color="red">Snowstorm appearance</font> (multiple hypoechoic foci and enlarged uterus) is the classic finding for CHM.
* In the biochemical evaluation, <font color="red">Serum β-HCG >100,000 IU/L</font> is seen in approximately 46% of CHM cases but is less common in PHM.

### TOPIC: MANAGEMENT AND SURVEILLANCE OF HYDATIDIFORM MOLE

| Intervention | Details / Indications |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Treatment** | Suction Dilation and Curettage (D&C) |
| **Sterilization Option** | Hysterectomy (if fertility is not desired) |
| **Prophylactic Chemotherapy**| Not routine; only if follow-up is unreliable and risk is high |
| **Standard Surveillance** | Weekly β-HCG until 3 normal values; then monthly for 6 months |
| **Contraception** | OCPs preferred; Avoid IUD until β-HCG normalization |

* In the surgical management of HM, <font color="red">Suction Dilation and Curettage (D&C)</font> is the preferred method for uterine evacuation.
* For patients who have completed childbearing, <font color="red">Hysterectomy</font> reduces the risk of postmolar GTN but does NOT eliminate the risk of distant metastases.
* Regarding anesthetic risk, <font color="red">Trophoblastic emboli</font> can cause acute respiratory distress during the evacuation of a large molar uterus.
* In Rh-negative patients, <font color="red">Rho(D) immune globulin</font> must be administered after molar evacuation to prevent sensitization.
* Regarding pharmacological support during D&C, <font color="red">Oxytocin infusion</font> is used to increase uterine tone and reduce bleeding, typically continued postoperatively.
* In the post-evacuation protocol, <font color="red">Serial β-HCG monitoring</font> is mandatory to detect the development of postmolar Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN).
* For standard surveillance, <font color="red">Serum β-HCG</font> must be checked weekly until normalization (<5 mIU/mL), followed by monthly monitoring for 6 months.
* Regarding family planning during follow-up, <font color="red">Reliable contraception (specifically OCPs)</font> is required to avoid pregnancy, which would confuse β-HCG monitoring.
* In the context of contraception, use of an <font color="red">Intrauterine Device (IUD)</font> should be avoided until β-HCG levels are normal due to the risk of uterine perforation.

### TOPIC: GESTATIONAL TROPHOBLASTIC NEOPLASIA (GTN) – PATHOLOGY AND METASTASIS

| Type | Pathological Hallmark | Clinical Behavior |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <font color="red">Invasive Mole</font> | Presence of **Villi** invading myometrium | Most common GTN following a mole. |
| <font color="red">Choriocarcinoma</font> | **No Villi**; sheets of anaplastic trophoblasts | Highly metastatic; follows term pregnancy or abortion. |
| <font color="red">PSTT</font> | **Intermediate trophoblasts** only; low β-HCG | Chemoresistant; late relapse potential. |
| <font color="red">ETT</font> | **Intermediate trophoblasts**; nodular growth | Rare variant; highly chemoresistant. |

* In the definition of <font color="red">Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN)</font>, the term denotes a disease state where there is biochemical evidence (abnormal β-HCG) of malignancy.
* Regarding the antecedent pregnancy, <font color="red">Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN)</font> follows a hydatidiform mole in 50% of cases, and abortion or term pregnancy in the other 50%.
* In the pathology of <font color="red">Invasive Mole</font>, the unique feature that distinguishes it from choriocarcinoma is the presence of chorionic villi.
* Regarding metastatic spread, <font color="red">Choriocarcinoma</font> has a significant propensity for early vascular infiltration and hematogenous dissemination.
* In the context of metastasis, the <font color="red">Lung</font> is the most common and often the first organ involved in metastatic GTN.
* For diagnostic procedures, <font color="red">Image-guided biopsy</font> of suspected metastatic GTN is contraindicated due to the risk of massive, uncontrollable hemorrhage of vascular lesions.
* Regarding the clinical presentation of metastasis, <font color="red">Hemoptysis</font> suggests lung involvement, while neurologic symptoms suggest brain metastasis.
* In the pathology of <font color="red">Placental Site Trophoblastic Tumor (PSTT)</font>, the tumor is composed almost entirely of intermediate trophoblasts and lacks syncytiotrophoblasts.
* Regarding tumor markers for PSTT/ETT, <font color="red">Serum β-HCG</font> is NOT a sensitive marker and is often only modestly elevated (<2,000 mIU/mL).
* For immunohistochemistry, <font color="red">Human Placental Lactogen (HPL)</font> is a characteristic marker for PSTT.
* For diagnostic staining in <font color="red">Epithelioid Trophoblastic Tumor (ETT)</font>, p63 and inhibin A are useful markers.

### TOPIC: GTN DIAGNOSIS, STAGING, AND SCORING

* In the biochemical diagnosis of <font color="red">Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN)</font>, one criterion is a plateau of β-HCG (±10%) for four measurements over 3 weeks (Days 1, 7, 14, 21).
* Another diagnostic criterion for <font color="red">Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia (GTN)</font> is a rise in serum β-HCG >10% for three consecutive weekly measurements over 2 weeks (Days 1, 7, 14).
* A <font color="red">Serum β-HCG level that remains detectable for 6 months</font> or more after molar evacuation is a diagnostic criterion for GTN.
* In FIGO staging, <font color="red">Stage I GTN</font> is disease confined strictly to the uterus.
* In FIGO staging, <font color="red">Stage II GTN</font> extends outside the uterus but is limited to genital structures (vagina, vulva, adnexa).
* In FIGO staging, <font color="red">Stage III GTN</font> denotes lung metastasis, with or without genital tract involvement.
* In FIGO staging, <font color="red">Stage IV GTN</font> involves all other metastatic sites, such as the brain, liver, or kidneys.
* Regarding prognosis, the <font color="red">WHO Prognostic Score</font> classifies patients as Low Risk if the score is 6 or lower.
* Regarding prognosis, the <font color="red">WHO Prognostic Score</font> classifies patients as High Risk if the score is 7 or higher.

| **High-Risk (Score ≥7)** | Combination chemotherapy (EMA/CO) |
| **Ultra-High Risk (Score ≥13)**| Low-dose induction EP (etoposide/cisplatin) then EMA/CO |
| **PSTT and ETT** | Hysterectomy (Primary); multiagent chemo (Adjuvant) |

* In the treatment of <font color="red">Low-risk GTN</font>, single-agent chemotherapy (usually Methotrexate or Actinomycin D) is the standard of care.
* Regarding Methotrexate toxicity, <font color="red">Folinic acid (Leucovorin)</font> is administered to counteract the toxic effects of Methotrexate on normal rapidly dividing cells.
* In the management of <font color="red">High-risk GTN</font>, the preferred multi-agent regimen is EMA/CO (Etoposide, Methotrexate, Actinomycin D, Cyclophosphamide, and Oncovin/Vincristine).
* Regarding <font color="red">EMA/CO administration</font>, the EMA course (Days 1 and 2) alternates with the CO course (Day 8) every week.
* In the management of <font color="red">PSTT and ETT</font>, hysterectomy is the primary treatment because these tumors are considered chemoresistant.
* For advanced <font color="red">PSTT/ETT</font>, a "sandwich approach" involving chemotherapy, surgery, and more chemotherapy is often employed.
* In metastatic disease management, <font color="red">Radiotherapy</font> is an adjunctive treatment used specifically for brain and liver metastases to achieve hemostatic and tumoricidal effects.
* Regarding the <font color="red">Quiescent type of GTN</font>, it is characterized by low-level β-HCG plateaus that should be monitored without active chemotherapy as it is non-responsive to drugs.
* In the context of <font color="red">Ultra-high risk GTN</font> (WHO score ≥13), low-dose induction chemotherapy is used first to prevent multi-organ failure from rapid tumor lysis.

---

### TOPIC: FOLLOW-UP AND FUTURE PREGNANCIES

* After achieving β-HCG remission, <font color="red">GTN Surveillance</font> for low-risk disease involves monthly monitoring for 12 months.
* For <font color="red">High-risk GTN Surveillance</font>, monitoring is done every 2 weeks for the first 3 weeks, then monthly for at least 2 years.
* Regarding future fertility, women who complete chemotherapy for GTN are advised to <font color="red">Delay pregnancy for 12 months</font> using reliable contraception.
* In <font color="red">Subsequent pregnancies</font> after GTD, early sonographic evaluation is recommended to confirm a normal pregnancy.
* After delivery in a patient with a history of GTD, <font color="red">Histopathologic exam of the placenta</font> and a 6-week postpartum β-HCG check are required.
* Regarding long-term risks, the <font color="red">Risk of relapse</font> for GTN beyond 1 year of remission is less than 1%.

---

### COMPARISON AND DIFFERENTIATION CLINICAL PEARLS (EXAM FOCUS)

1. Characterized by **VILLI** on histology, <font color="red">Invasive Mole</font> is the most common type following a molar pregnancy.
2. Characterized by a **LACK OF VILLI** and a dimorphic cell population, <font color="red">Choriocarcinoma</font> is the most common type following a term pregnancy or abortion.
3. In differentiating markers, <font color="red">Choriocarcinoma</font> stains strongly for β-HCG, while <font color="red">PSTT</font> stains strongly for Human Placental Lactogen (HPL).
4. Regarding ultrasound findings, <font color="red">Complete Hydatidiform Mole</font> shows a "snowstorm" pattern, whereas <font color="red">Partial Hydatidiform Mole</font> may show a "Swiss cheese" placenta or focal cystic spaces.
5. In terms of ploidy, <font color="red">CHM</font> is diploid (mostly 46,XX), while <font color="red">PHM</font> is triploid (mostly 69,XXY).
6. Regarding immunostaining, <font color="red">p57</font> is negative in CHM but positive in PHM and non-molar hydropic abortions.
7. In distinguishing GTN types, <font color="red">PSTT and ETT</font> are chemoresistant and require surgery, whereas <font color="red">Invasive Mole and Choriocarcinoma</font> are chemosensitive and treated primarily with drugs.
8. Regarding β-HCG levels, <font color="red">Choriocarcinoma</font> typically presents with very high levels (>10,000), while <font color="red">PSTT/ETT</font> present with low levels (<2,000).
9. In the context of risk scores, <font color="red">Low-risk GTN</font> (≤6) receives single-agent therapy, whereas <font color="red">High-risk GTN</font> (≥7) receives multi-agent EMA/CO.
10. Regarding the origin of cells, <font color="red">PSTT</font> arises from the placental implantation site (intermediate trophoblasts), while <font color="red">ETT</font> arises from the chorionic laeve (intermediate trophoblasts).
11. In the context of hysterectomy, it is the **PRIMARY** treatment for <font color="red">PSTT/ETT</font>, but only an **ADJUNCTIVE** treatment for <font color="red">Invasive Mole/Choriocarcinoma</font>.
12. Regarding gross pathology, <font color="red">PSTT</font> typically forms a solid polypoid mass, while <font color="red">ETT</font> presents as a nodular lesion that can involve the lower uterine segment or cervix.
13. In histology, <font color="red">ETT</font> is monomorphic with small round cells and frequent calcification, while <font color="red">PSTT</font> is monomorphic with large pleomorphic cells.
14. Regarding vascularity, <font color="red">Choriocarcinoma</font> is highly vascular and prone to massive hemorrhage, whereas <font color="red">PSTT</font> is less at risk for vascular invasion.
15. In terms of metastatic pattern, <font color="red">Choriocarcinoma</font> spreads via the blood (hematogenous), while <font color="red">Invasive Mole</font> tends to be more locally aggressive into the myometrium.
16. In terms of clinical suspicion, a <font color="red">Partial Mole</font> is often misdiagnosed as an incomplete or missed abortion until the pathology report is received.
17. Regarding the "Theca Lutein Cyst," it is a **PERTINENT POSITIVE** finding in 25% of <font color="red">CHM</font> cases but a **PERTINENT NEGATIVE** in most <font color="red">PHM</font> cases.
18. Regarding staging, <font color="red">Stage III</font> is defined by lung metastasis, while <font color="red">Stage IV</font> includes brain and liver metastasis (worse prognosis).
19. In terms of β-HCG tracking, a **rising level** indicates GTN, whereas a **plateauing level** at very low values without progression might indicate <font color="red">Quiescent GTN</font>.
20. In pharmacological choice, <font color="red">Actinomycin D</font> is often used in low-risk GTN if the patient is resistant to or cannot tolerate Methotrexate.
