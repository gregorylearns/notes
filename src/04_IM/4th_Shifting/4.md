| MEGALOBLASTIC ANEMIA: OVERVIEW |                                                                                                                                                              |
| :----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Pathogenesis**               | Result of defective DNA synthesis caused by lack of Cobalamin (B12) or Folate; characterized by maturation defects and ineffective erythropoiesis.           |
| **Hallmark Findings**          | Hypercellular bone marrow, hypersegmented neutrophils, and oval macrocytes.                                                                                  |
| **Differential Diagnosis**     | Lack of Cobalamin, Folate metabolism problems, Antifolate medications (e.g., Methotrexate), or deficiency refractory to treatment.                           |
| **Key Medication**             | <font color="red">Methotrexate</font> is a cDMARD known to notoriously cause low folate; its antidote is <font color="red">Folinic Acid (Leucovorin)</font>. |

* [Megaloblastic Anemia Overview] <font color="red">Bone Marrow</font> is usually hypercellular in megaloblastic anemia, and the resulting anemia is based on ineffective erythropoiesis.
* [Megaloblastic Anemia Overview] The classic clinical association for <font color="red">Megaloblastic Anemia</font> includes Vitamin B12 deficiency, hypersegmented neutrophils, liver problems, and folic acid deficiency.
* [Antifolate Medications] <font color="red">Methotrexate</font> is a conventional synthetic Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drug (cDMARD) used in autoimmune conditions and cancer that often causes folate deficiency.
* [Antifolate Medications] <font color="red">Folinic Acid (Leucovorin)</font> is the specific antidote used to reverse the effects of Methotrexate toxicity.

| COBALAMIN (VITAMIN B12) CHARACTERISTICS |                                                                                                                                                      |
| :-------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Chemical Structure**                  | Contains a cobalt atom at the center of a corrin ring.                                                                                               |
| **Metabolic Form 1**                    | <font color="red">Adenosylcobalamin (Ado-B12)</font>: Located in mitochondria; required for conversion of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA.         |
| **Metabolic Form 2**                    | <font color="red">Methylcobalamin</font>: Located in cytoplasm/plasma; converts homocysteine to methionine (deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia). |
| **Dietary Sources**                     | Synthesized solely by microorganisms; found in foods of animal origin (meat, fish, dairy).                                                           |
| **Storage & Loss**                      | Stores (2-3 mg) last 3-4 years; daily losses/requirements are 1-3 ug.                                                                                |

* [Cobalamin Metabolism] <font color="red">Adenosylcobalamin (Ado-B12)</font> is the mitochondrial form of B12; a deficiency leads to an increase in methylmalonic acid (MMA).
* [Cobalamin Metabolism] <font color="red">Methylcobalamin</font> is the cytoplasmic form of B12 involved in converting homocysteine to methionine; a deficiency leads to elevated homocysteine levels.
* [Cobalamin Absorption] <font color="red">Intrinsic Factor (IF)</font> is a glycoprotein produced by gastric parietal cells (gene on chromosome 11q13) required for the active absorption of B12 in the ileum.
* [Cobalamin Absorption] <font color="red">Cubilin</font> is the specific receptor in the ileum that mediates the endocytosis of the IF-B12 complex.
* [Cobalamin Transport] <font color="red">Transcobalamin II (TC II)</font> is the primary protein responsible for delivering cobalamin to tissues like the bone marrow and placenta.
* [Cobalamin Transport] <font color="red">Transcobalamin I (Haptocorrin)</font> binds approximately 2/3 of circulating B12 but does not deliver it to tissues.

| COBALAMIN DEFICIENCY CAUSES |
| :--- |
| **Dietary** | Vegans/Strict vegetarians are at highest risk. |
| **Gastric (IF Lack)** | Pernicious Anemia (autoimmune), Gastrectomy (loss of parietal cells), Congenital IF deficiency. |
| **Intestinal** | Stagnant Loop Syndrome (bacterial overgrowth), Ileal Resection (especially ≥1.2 m), Tropical Sprue, Diphyllobothrium latum (fish tapeworm). |
| **Drugs** | Metformin, PPIs, Nitrous Oxide, Alcohol, Colchicine. |

* [Gastric Causes] <font color="red">Pernicious Anemia</font> is an autoimmune condition characterized by gastric atrophy, loss of parietal cells, and a severe lack of Intrinsic Factor.
* [Gastric Causes] <font color="red">Juvenile Pernicious Anemia</font> presents in older children with gastric atrophy and IF antibodies, often associated with autoimmune endocrinopathies.
* [Gastric Causes] <font color="red">Gastrectomy</font> (total or partial) leads to B12 deficiency because the removal of the gastric antrum/body eliminates the parietal cells that produce Intrinsic Factor.
* [Ileal Causes] <font color="red">Ileal Resection</font> involving ≥1.2 meters of the terminal ileum results in significant B12 malabsorption as this is the primary site of uptake.
* [Intestinal Causes] <font color="red">Intestinal Stagnant Loop Syndrome</font> involves bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine where fecal organisms consume B12, causing deficiency.
* [Infectious Causes] <font color="red">Diphyllobothrium latum</font> (fish tapeworm) causes B12 deficiency because the parasite competes with the host to consume the vitamin.
* [Metabolic Defects] <font color="red">Nitrous Oxide (N2O)</font> irreversibly oxidizes methylcobalamin to an inactive precursor, inactivating methionine synthase and causing rapid megaloblastic changes.
* [Rare Syndromes] <font color="red">Imerslund-Grasbeck Syndrome</font> is a rare autosomal recessive condition characterized by selective B12 malabsorption and proteinuria in infants.

| FOLATE (VITAMIN B9) CHARACTERISTICS |
| :--- |
| **Dietary Sources** | Liver, yeast, spinach, greens, and nuts; easily destroyed by heating. |
| **Storage & Loss** | Stores (10 mg) last only 3-4 months; daily adult requirement is 100-200 ug. |
| **Absorption site** | Rapidly absorbed in the proximal small intestine (duodenum and jejunum). |
| **High Demand States**| Pregnancy (600 ug/day), Lactation, Prematurity, Chronic hemolytic anemias. |

* [Folate Deficiency] <font color="red">Dietary Folate</font> deficiency is common in populations with limited access to fresh produce or in infants fed solely on goat's milk.
* [Folate Malabsorption] <font color="red">PCFT Mutation</font> causes congenital selective folate malabsorption, leading to megaloblastic anemia and CNS abnormalities like convulsions.
* [Folate Requirements] <font color="red">Pregnancy</font> increases the folate requirement to 600 µg/day to support fetal transfer and prevent neural tube defects.
* [Folate Utilization] <font color="red">Chronic Hemolytic Anemias</font> (e.g., Sickle Cell Disease) lead to folate deficiency due to increased red cell turnover and folate demand.
* [Folate Loss] <font color="red">Long-Term Dialysis</font> patients are at risk for folate deficiency because the vitamin is lost through the dialysis membrane.

| HEMATOLOGIC & CLINICAL FINDINGS (MEGALOBLASTIC) |
| :--- |
| **Peripheral Blood** | <font color="red">Oval macrocytes</font>, <font color="red">Hypersegmented neutrophils</font> (early/specific), Anisocytosis, Poikilocytosis. |
| **Bone Marrow** | Hypercellular with primitive cells; apoptosis of mature forms (ineffective hematopoiesis). |
| **B12 Neuro Symptoms**| Bilateral peripheral neuropathy, subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord, dementia. |
| **Folate Neuro Symptoms**| Neural tube defects (spina bifida) in neonates. |

* [Hematology] <font color="red">Hypersegmented Neutrophils</font> (defined as having >5 lobes) are one of the earliest and most specific peripheral blood findings in megaloblastic anemia.
* [Hematology] <font color="red">Oval Macrocytes</font> are large, oval-shaped red blood cells that result from impaired DNA synthesis and fewer cell divisions.
* [Ineffective Hematopoiesis] <font color="red">Unconjugated Bilirubin</font> and LDH may be elevated in megaloblastic anemia due to the premature death of nucleated red cells in the bone marrow.
* [Clinical Presentation] <font color="red">Glossitis</font> (a beefy red tongue) is a classic physical exam finding in severe megaloblastic anemia.
* [Neurological Impact] <font color="red">Vitamin B12 Deficiency</font> is uniquely associated with demyelinating neurological damage, unlike pure folate deficiency.
* [Treatment Safety] <font color="red">Cobalamin Deficiency</font> must be ruled out before giving large doses of folic acid, as folate can correct the anemia but allow irreversible B12 neuropathy to progress.

| DIAGNOSIS OF MEGALOBLASTIC ANEMIA |
| :--- |
| **Serum B12** | Borderline is 74-148 pmol/L (100-200 ng/L); deficiency is <74 pmol/L (100 ng/L). |
| **Serum Folate** | Low in deficiency but can be "raised" in B12 deficiency due to the methyl-THF trap. |
| **Red Cell Folate** | More stable measure of body folate stores; less affected by recent diet. |
| **MMA** | Significantly elevated in Vitamin B12 deficiency; normal in folate deficiency. |
| **Homocysteine**| Elevated in both Vitamin B12 and Folate deficiency. |

* [Laboratory Diagnosis] <font color="red">Methylmalonic Acid (MMA)</font> is the specific biochemical marker used to differentiate B12 deficiency from folate deficiency.
* [Laboratory Diagnosis] <font color="red">Serum Folate</font> may rise in severe B12 deficiency due to a block in the conversion of MTHF to THF (the "folate trap").
* [Diagnostic Pitfall] <font color="red">Intrinsic Factor Antibodies</font> in serum can cause false-normal serum cobalamin levels in up to 50% of Pernicious Anemia cases.

| HEMOLYTIC ANEMIA: CLINICAL & LAB FEATURES |
| :--- |
| **Clinical Triad** | Jaundice (unconjugated), Pallor, Splenomegaly. |
| **Chronic Signs** | Skeleton changes (skull bossing/chipmunk facies), Pigment gallstones. |
| **Lab Markers** | Low Hb, High Reticulocytes, High LDH, High Unconjugated Bilirubin. |
| **Intravascular HA** | Absent or very low <font color="red">Haptoglobin</font>, Hemoglobinuria. |

* [Hemolysis Overview] <font color="red">Jaundice</font> in hemolytic anemia is primarily due to an increase in unconjugated/indirect bilirubin from RBC breakdown.
* [Hemolysis Overview] <font color="red">Splenomegaly</font> is a classic finding in hemolytic anemia as the spleen is a major site of extravascular red cell destruction.
* [Chronic Hemolysis] <font color="red">Skull Bossing</font> occurs in chronic hemolytic states as the bone marrow expands into the skull to compensate for anemia.
* [Chronic Hemolysis] <font color="red">Pigment Gallstones</font> (calcium bilirubinate) are common in chronic hemolytic patients due to high bilirubin turnover.

| INHERITED HEMOLYTIC ANEMIAS (MEMBRANE & ENZYME) |
| :--- |
| **Hereditary Spherocytosis**| Most common inherited membrane defect; usually ANK1 (Ankyrin) mutation; MCHC >34. |
| **G6PD Deficiency** | Most common enzyme deficiency; X-linked; triggered by oxidative stress (Fava beans, drugs). |
| **Pyruvate Kinase Def.** | Most common glycolytic enzyme deficiency; Autosomal recessive. |
| **SAO** | Southeast Asia Ovalocytosis; Band 3 mutation; common in the Philippines; malaria protection. |

* [Hereditary Spherocytosis] <font color="red">MCHC >34</font> g/dL on a standard blood count is a strong clinical clue for the diagnosis of Hereditary Spherocytosis.
* [Hereditary Spherocytosis] <font color="red">Ankyrin</font> (ANK1) is the most common protein mutation responsible for Hereditary Spherocytosis.
* [Hereditary Spherocytosis] <font color="red">Splenectomy</font> is the curative treatment for moderate-to-severe HS, but requires prior vaccination against encapsulated bacteria (*S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, N. meningitidis*).
* [G6PD Deficiency] <font color="red">G6PD Deficiency</font> provides the reducing agent NADPH required to protect RBCs from oxidative damage (e.g., from naphthalene or sulfonamides).
* [Pyruvate Kinase Deficiency] <font color="red">Pyruvate Kinase Deficiency</font> is often better tolerated than other anemias because of an increase in 2,3-BPG (DPG), which shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the right.

| AUTOIMMUNE HEMOLYTIC ANEMIA (AIHA) COMPARISON |
| :--- |
| **Warm AIHA** | IgG antibodies; optimal at 37°C; Extravascular (Spleen) hemolysis; Spherocytes. |
| **Cold Agglutinin (CAD)**| IgM antibodies; optimal at 4-30°C; Liver/Intravascular hemolysis; Agglutination on smear. |
| **Treatment (Warm)** | Steroids (Prednisone), Rituximab, Splenectomy. |
| **Treatment (Cold)** | Cold avoidance, Rituximab, Sutimlimab (C1s inhibitor). |

* [Warm AIHA] <font color="red">IgG Autoantibodies</font> in Warm AIHA coat red cells and lead to their removal by Fc receptor-bearing macrophages, largely in the spleen.
* [Cold Agglutinin Disease] <font color="red">IgM Autoantibodies</font> bind to RBCs in peripheral cold areas (fingers/toes) and cause strong complement activation.
* [Cold Agglutinin Disease] <font color="red">Rituximab</font> is the treatment of choice for Cold Agglutinin Disease because steroids and splenectomy are generally ineffective.
* [AIHA Diagnosis] <font color="red">Direct Coombs Test</font> is positive for IgG +/- C3 in Warm AIHA, but positive mainly for C3 in Cold Agglutinin Disease.

| PAROXYSMAL NOCTURNAL HEMOGLOBINURIA (PNH) |
| :--- |
| **Pathogenesis** | Somatic mutation in PIGA gene; lack of GPI-anchor proteins. |
| **Protective Proteins** | Missing <font color="red">CD55</font> (DAF) and <font color="red">CD59</font> (MIRL), leading to complement-mediated lysis. |
| **Classic Triad** | (1) Intravascular hemolysis, (2) Pancytopenia, (3) Venous thrombosis. |
| **Gold Standard Test** | <font color="red">Flow Cytometry</font> (identifying deficiency of CD55/CD59). |
| **Treatment** | <font color="red">Eculizumab</font> (C5 complement inhibitor). |

* [PNH Triad] <font color="red">Venous Thrombosis</font> is the most common cause of death in PNH, often occurring in unusual sites like the hepatic or cerebral veins.
* [PNH Pathophysiology] <font color="red">CD59</font> deficiency is the primary reason for intravascular hemolysis in PNH because it fails to inhibit the membrane attack complex (MAC).
* [PNH Presentation] <font color="red">Hemoglobinuria</font> in PNH often presents as dark or "cola-colored" urine, classically noted in the first morning void.
* [PNH Complications] <font color="red">Budd-Chiari Syndrome</font> is a high-yield association with PNH due to portal/hepatic vein thrombosis.

| EXAM COMPARISON TABLE: DIFFERENTIATING SIMILAR ENTITIES |
| :--- |
| 1. [B12 vs Folate Stores] <font color="red">Vitamin B12</font> stores last 3-4 years, while <font color="red">Folate</font> stores last only 3-4 months.
| 2. [Neuropathy] <font color="red">Neurological symptoms</font> are common in B12 deficiency (peripheral neuropathy, spinal cord degeneration) but absent in folate deficiency.
| 3. [MMA Specificity] <font color="red">Methylmalonic Acid (MMA)</font> is elevated ONLY in B12 deficiency; <font color="red">Homocysteine</font> is elevated in both B12 and folate deficiency.
| 4. [Absorption Site] <font color="red">B12</font> is absorbed in the terminal ileum (requires IF), while <font color="red">Folate</font> is absorbed in the duodenum/jejunum.
| 5. [Inheritance] <font color="red">Hereditary Spherocytosis</font> is usually Autosomal Dominant, while <font color="red">Pyruvate Kinase Deficiency</font> is Autosomal Recessive.
| 6. [AIHA Antibody] <font color="red">Warm AIHA</font> involves IgG (the "Warm Great" antibody), while <font color="red">Cold Agglutinin Disease</font> involves IgM (the "Cold Miser" antibody).
| 7. [AIHA Destruction Site] <font color="red">Warm AIHA</font> destruction happens mainly in the spleen; <font color="red">Cold AIHA</font> destruction happens mainly in the liver via Kupffer cells.
| 8. [Steroid Response] <font color="red">Prednisone</font> is effective for Warm AIHA but ineffective for Cold Agglutinin Disease.
| 9. [Spherocytes vs Agglutination] <font color="red">Spherocytes</font> are seen in Warm AIHA and Hereditary Spherocytosis; <font color="red">Agglutination</font> (clumping) is seen in Cold Agglutinin Disease.
| 10. [Enzyme Pathways] <font color="red">Pyruvate Kinase</font> is part of the glycolytic pathway (Embden-Meyerhof); <font color="red">G6PD</font> is part of the hexose monophosphate shunt.
| 11. [Neonatal Jaundice] <font color="red">Pyruvate Kinase Deficiency</font> causes persistent neonatal jaundice; <font color="red">G6PD Deficiency</font> causes acute hemolytic episodes triggered by stress.
| 12. [Haptoglobin] <font color="red">Haptoglobin</font> levels are specifically used to identify intravascular hemolysis (it will be depleted/absent).
| 13. [Pernicious vs Juvenile PA] <font color="red">Pernicious Anemia</font> features parietal cell antibodies; <font color="red">Juvenile Pernicious Anemia</font> often lacks parietal cell antibodies but has IF antibodies and endocrine issues.
| 14. [B12 vs Folate Drugs] <font color="red">Metformin</font> is associated with B12 deficiency; <font color="red">Methotrexate</font> is associated with folate deficiency.
| 15. [Treatment Priority] In megaloblastic anemia, one must treat <font color="red">B12 deficiency</font> first (or simultaneously) with folate to prevent the "masking" of neurological disease.
| 16. [PNH vs Others] <font color="red">PNH</font> is the only hemolytic anemia discussed that is ACQUIRED via a somatic mutation (PIGA), rather than being inherited or purely autoimmune.
| 17. [G6PD vs PNH] <font color="red">G6PD</font> acts like an "episode" triggered by beans/drugs; <font color="red">PNH</font> acts like a "chronic clone" causing constant intravascular lysis and risk of clots.
| 18. [IF Sources] <font color="red">Parietal cells</font> produce Intrinsic Factor; <font color="red">Salivary glands</font> produce Haptocorrin (R-binder) which protects B12 in the stomach.
| 19. [MMA Caveat] <font color="red">Renal Failure</font> can cause a false elevation of Methylmalonic Acid (MMA) even without B12 deficiency.
| 20. [Transfusion Goal] In <font color="red">PNH</font>, transfusion is avoided unless hemoglobin is <7 g/dL to minimize iron overload and autoantibody formation.
| 21. [Folinic vs Folic] <font color="red">Folinic acid (Leucovorin)</font> is already reduced and bypasses DHF reductase; <font color="red">Folic acid</font> requires DHF reductase to be activated.
| 22. [B12 Transport] <font color="red">Transcobalamin II</font> is the "delivery truck" to tissues; <font color="red">Transcobalamin I</font> is the "warehouse" for storage in the blood.
| 23. [Coombs Comparison] <font color="red">Direct Coombs</font> tests for antibodies already on the patient's RBCs; a positive result for C3 only is highly suggestive of CAD.
| 24. [Splenectomy Success] <font color="red">Splenectomy</font> is highly effective for HS and Warm AIHA because the spleen is the primary site of destruction for IgG-coated cells or spherocytes.
| 25. [SAO Feature] <font color="red">Southeast Asia Ovalocytosis (SAO)</font> is likely a malaria-protective polymorphism and often asymptomatic in heterozygotes.

### ANEMIA AND POLYCYTHEMIA: PHYSIOLOGY AND CLINICAL EVALUATION

| Topic Component | Anemia Overview & Physiology |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Pathogenesis** | Inadequate <font color="red">red cell mass</font> generally results from either decreased RBC production (hypoproliferative) or increased RBC destruction/loss (hemolysis or bleeding). |
| **Primary Regulator** | <font color="red">Erythropoietin (EPO)</font> is the primary hormone regulating RBC production, produced mainly by peritubular capillary lining cells in the kidney in response to hypoxia. |
| **Critical Elements** | <font color="red">Erythropoiesis</font> requires normal renal EPO production, a functioning erythroid marrow, and adequate substrates for hemoglobin synthesis (primarily iron). |
| **RBC Kinetics** | <font color="red">Mature RBCs</font> have a lifespan of 100–120 days, with 0.8–1% of the total mass being replaced daily. |
| **Lab Standards** | <font color="red">Hemoglobin (Hgb)</font> concentration is clinically preferred over hematocrit because hematocrit is a calculated value and generally less accurate. |

*   Context: Anemia Definitions. The <font color="red">WHO definition of anemia</font> is a Hemoglobin level of <13 g/dL in men and <12 g/dL in women.
*   Context: Normal Hemoglobin Ranges. <font color="red">Normal Hemoglobin values</font> for adult males range from 13.5–17.5 g/dL, while for adult females the range is 12–15 g/dL.
*   Context: Physiology of EPO. <font color="red">Erythropoietin (EPO)</font> prevents the apoptosis of erythroid progenitor cells and promotes their proliferation and maturation.
*   Context: EPO Regulation. <font color="red">Hypoxia-Inducible Factor (HIF-1α)</font> is the key regulator of EPO; in normoxia, it is degraded by the proteasome, but in hypoxia, it translocates to the nucleus to increase EPO gene expression.
*   Context: Marrow Response. An <font color="red">increased EPO stimulation</font> can increase RBC production 4–5 times the normal rate within 1–2 weeks, provided iron stores are adequate.
*   Context: RBC Morphology. A <font color="red">mature RBC</font> is approximately 8 µm in diameter, anucleate, biconcave/discoid in shape, and highly deformable to pass through microcirculation.

### COMPENSATORY MECHANISMS TO ANEMIA

| Mechanism | Physiological Change | Clinical Correlate |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Oxygen Affinity** | <font color="red">Decreased O2 Affinity</font> via increased 2,3-DPG. | Right shift in the oxygen-dissociation curve; common in chronic anemia and high altitude. |
| **Metabolism** | <font color="red">Pasteur Effect</font>: Upregulation of glycolysis. | Conversion to less efficient anaerobic glycolysis for energy during hypoxia. |
| **Perfusion** | <font color="red">Blood Shunting</font> to vital organs. | Vasomotor activity diverts blood from skin, kidneys, and gut to the brain and myocardium. |
| **Hemodynamics** | <font color="red">Increased Cardiac Output</font>. | Occurs when Hgb <7 g/dL; results in tachycardia, flow murmurs, and pounding pulses. |
| **Pulmonary** | <font color="red">Increased Respiratory Function</font>. | Increased respiratory rate to maximize O2 uptake; presents as exertional dyspnea/orthopnea. |

*   Context: Cardiac Compensation. <font color="red">Increased resting cardiac output</font> in anemia typically does not occur until the hemoglobin concentration falls below 7 g/dL.
*   Context: Physical Examination. <font color="red">Hemodynamic "flow" murmurs</font> and bruits may be heard over the jugular vein, closed eye, or parietal region of the skull in severe anemia.
*   Context: Hypermetabolism in Cancer. The <font color="red">Warburg Effect</font> is a characteristic of malignant cells where they utilize excess glucose for conversion to lactate even in the presence of oxygen.
*   Context: Auditory Symptoms. <font color="red">Tinnitus</font> described as "roaring in the ears" can be a clinical sign of increased cardiac output due to severe anemia.
*   Context: Ischemic Complications. <font color="red">Angina pectoris</font> may supervene during anemia if the oxygen demand of the myocardium exceeds the supply, especially in patients with existing coronary artery disease.

### PRACTICAL LABORATORY APPROACH AND RETICULOCYTE COUNT

| Step | Measurement | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Confirm Anemia** | Hgb and Hct levels. | Determines if the red cell mass is truly decreased below WHO cut-offs. |
| **2. Production vs Loss** | <font color="red">Reticulocyte Count</font>. | Distinguishes hypoproliferative (<2%) from hemolytic/bleeding (>2%) states. |
| **3. Size/Morphology** | <font color="red">MCV</font> & Peripheral Smear. | Categories: Microcytic (<80), Normocytic (80-100), Macrocytic (>100). |
| **4. Iron Status** | Serum Iron, Ferritin, TIBC. | Differentiates IDA, Inflammation, and SID/Thalassemia. |

*   Context: Relative Anemia. <font color="red">Dilutional (Relative) Anemia</font> is characterized by a normal total red cell mass but an increased plasma volume, seen in pregnancy, athletes, and macroglobulinemia.
*   Context: Reticulocyte Life Span. <font color="red">Reticulocytes</font> are immature RBCs that contain ribosomal RNA and typically circulate for 1 day before maturing; they are identified using supravital dyes.
*   Context: Corrected Reticulocyte Count. The <font color="red">Absolute Reticulocyte Count</font> formula is: Retic Count % × (Patient Hct / Expected Hct).
*   Context: Bone Marrow Response. The <font color="red">Reticulocyte Production Index (RPI)</font> is calculated as (Absolute Retic Count / Maturation Time Correction); an RPI <2 indicates inadequate marrow response.
*   Context: Premature Release. <font color="red">Shift Cells (Polychromasia)</font> are reticulocytes released prematurely from the marrow that stay in circulation longer (2 days instead of 1), which can falsely elevate the retic count.
*   Context: Expected Response. In severe anemia (Hgb <10 g/dL), the <font color="red">reticulocyte production</font> should ideally increase 2–3 times the normal rate within approximately 10 days.

*   Context: Thalassemia in the Philippines. <font color="red">Thalassemias</font> are common in the Philippines and present with microcytic, hypochromic anemia despite normal iron stores; they should not be treated with iron.
*   Context: Sickle Cell Disease. <font color="red">Sickle Hemoglobinopathies</font> are uncommon in the Philippines but manifest with pain, splenic infarction, and increased risk for stroke and CKD.

*   Context: Transfusion in Comorbidity. <font color="red">Transfusion</font> should be considered for Hgb levels between 7–8 g/dL in patients with cardiovascular instability or between 8–10 g/dL in patients with an active MI.
*   Context: Active Bleeding. In cases of <font color="red">active bleeding</font>, transfusion may be required even if the current hemoglobin level appears normal at that moment.

### DIFFERENTIATION AND COMPARSION (THE CONFUSION FIXER)

1.  **IDA vs. AI:** In <font color="red">Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA)</font>, Ferritin is low and TIBC is high; in <font color="red">Anemia of Inflammation (AI)</font>, Ferritin is normal or high and TIBC is low.
2.  **Absolute vs. Relative Anemia:** <font color="red">Absolute anemia</font> involves a true decrease in red cell mass; <font color="red">Relative anemia</font> involves a normal red cell mass diluted by increased plasma volume (e.g., pregnancy).
3.  **Burr Cells vs. Spur Cells:** <font color="red">Burr cells (Echinocytes)</font> have regular, small projections and are seen in Uremia; <font color="red">Spur cells (Acanthocytes)</font> have irregular, thorn-like projections and are seen in Liver Disease.
4.  **MCV Categories:** <font color="red">Microcytic</font> is MCV <80 fL; <font color="red">Normocytic</font> is 80–100 fL; <font color="red">Macrocytic</font> is >100 fL.
5.  **Anisocytosis vs. Poikilocytosis:** <font color="red">Anisocytosis</font> is variation in size (measured by RDW); <font color="red">Poikilocytosis</font> is variation in shape.
6.  **RPI interpretation:** An <font color="red">RPI &lt2</font> suggests a hypoproliferative cause (production problem); an <font color="red">RPI >2–3</font> suggests hemolysis or acute blood loss (destruction/loss problem).
7.  **Megaloblastic vs. Non-megaloblastic Macrocytosis:** <font color="red">Megaloblastic macrocytosis</font> features hypersegmented neutrophils (B12/Folate deficiency); <font color="red">Non-megaloblastic macrocytosis</font> does not (Alcohol/Liver disease).
8.  **Pasteur vs. Warburg Effect:** The <font color="red">Pasteur effect</font> is a normal response to hypoxia (anaerobic glycolysis), while the <font color="red">Warburg effect</font> is malignant glycolysis occurring even with oxygen present.
9.  **Alpha vs. Beta Thalassemia Electrophoresis:** <font color="red">Beta-thalassemia</font> shows an abnormal pattern on hemoglobin electrophoresis; <font color="red">Alpha-thalassemia</font> often shows a normal electrophoresis pattern.
10. **Serum Iron vs. Ferritin:** <font color="red">Serum Iron</font> measures circulating iron; <font color="red">Serum Ferritin</font> is the best indicator of total body iron stores.
11. **Howell-Jolly vs. Pappenheimer Bodies:** <font color="red">Howell-Jolly bodies</font> are DNA remnants (Blue, Wright stain) seen in asplenia; they are distinct from iron-containing inclusions.
12. **Iron supplementation:** <font color="red">Ferrous sulfate</font> usually contains 60 mg elemental iron, while <font color="red">Sangobion</font> contains 100-140 mg; iron can cause constipation, so sorbitol is sometimes added.
13. **Target Cells (Codocytes) Differential:** While seen in <font color="red">Thalassemia</font>, Target Cells can also appear in liver disease and severe iron deficiency.
14. **High RDW:** A <font color="red">high RDW</font> implies a mixed population of cells (early deficiency states like IDA or B12 deficiency), whereas a <font color="red">normal RDW</font> with low MCV might suggest Thalassemia.
15. **Transfusion Thresholds:** Most stable patients use a <font color="red">7 g/dL cut-off</font>, but symptomatic CAD or MI patients require a higher threshold of <font color="red">8-10 g/dL</font>.
16. **Hypoproliferative causes:** <font color="red">Anemia of CKD</font> is primarily due to EPO deficiency; <font color="red">Anemia of AI</font> is primarily iron sequestration due to Hepcidin.
17. **Vitamin B12 vs Folate:** Both cause megaloblastic anemia, but B12 deficiency is often associated with lower serum B12 levels (normal 200-900 ng/L) and unique neurologic symptoms not mentioned in this text but standard in Harrison's.
18. **Hemoglobin vs Hematocrit Accuracy:** <font color="red">Hemoglobin</font> is directly measured; <font color="red">Hematocrit</font> can be misleading because it is affected by plasma volume and is a calculated value.
19. **WHO Anemia in Pregnancy:** Hemoglobin levels may naturally drop to <font color="red">11-12 g/dL</font> due to the dilution effect of increased plasma volume.

[APLASTIC ANEMIA (AA)]
- [AA - Epidemiology] <font color="red">Aplastic Anemia</font> affects men and women equally and has a biphasic age distribution (teens-twenties and older adults).
- [AA - Etiology] <font color="red">EBV (Epstein-Barr Virus)</font> is the most notorious virus associated with the development of aplastic anemia.
- [AA - Etiology] <font color="red">Chloramphenicol and Benzene (found in gasoline)</font> are drugs and chemicals that have a consistent and drastic association with aplastic anemia.
- [AA - Pathophysiology] The <font color="red">hallmark morphology of Aplastic Anemia</font> is the replacement of bone marrow hematopoietic tissue by fat (yellow marrow).
- [AA - Pathophysiology] <font color="red">CD34 cells</font> are significantly decreased in Aplastic Anemia, along with an absence of committed and progenitor cells.
- [AA - Clinical] <font color="red">Bleeding (petechiae, ecchymoses, mucosal)</font> is the most common early symptom of Aplastic Anemia due to low platelets.
- [AA - Clinical] <font color="red">Infection</font> is an unusual first symptom in Aplastic Anemia; if present early, other causes like HIV or autoimmune diseases should be ruled out.
- [AA - Clinical] <font color="red">Lymphadenopathy (LAD) and splenomegaly</font> are highly unlikely in Aplastic Anemia and should prompt consideration of other diagnoses.
- [AA - Clinical] <font color="red">Fanconi Anemia</font> is a constitutional form of Aplastic Anemia characterized by Café au lait spots and short stature.
- [AA - Clinical] <font color="red">Dyskeratosis Congenita</font> is a constitutional form of Aplastic Anemia characterized by peculiar nails, leukoplakia, and telomerase defects (early graying).
- [AA - Clinical Differentiation] <font color="red">Mucosal bleeding</font> (gums, nose, GI) suggests a platelet problem, while <font color="red">cavity bleeding</font> (joints/hemarthrosis, muscles) suggests a clotting factor deficiency.

- [AA - Labs] <font color="red">Absent reticulocytes</font> are expected in Aplastic Anemia due to bone marrow failure and lack of production.
- [AA - Labs] <font color="red">Immature myeloid forms</font> in the blood suggest leukemia or MDS rather than true Aplastic Anemia.
- [AA - Labs] <font color="red">Bone marrow in Aplastic Anemia</font> typically shows fat with hematopoietic cells occupying less than 25% of the space.

- [Myelophthisis - Clinical] <font color="red">Leucoerythroblastic smear</font> featuring immature red and white cells is a hallmark of Myelophthisic Anemia.
- [Myelophthisis - Clinical] <font color="red">Tear drop-shaped RBCs (Dacrocytes)</font> are characteristic of Myelophthisis as cells squeeze through fibrotic marrow or the spleen.
- [Myelophthisis - Diagnosis] A <font color="red">DRY tap</font> during bone marrow aspiration is common in Myelophthisic Anemia because the marrow is replaced by fiber rather than fluid.

[POLYCYTHEMIA VERA (PV)]
- [PV - Definition] <font color="red">Polycythemia Vera</font> is the most common myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN), characterized by the overproduction of all myeloid cells without a physiologic stimulus.
- [PV - Etiology] The <font color="red">JAK2 V617F mutation (*)</font> involves a valine to phenylalanine substitution, causing constitutive tyrosine kinase signaling and EPO-independent erythropoiesis.
- [PV - Clinical] <font color="red">Aquagenic pruritus</font> is a classic symptom of PV where the patient feels itchy after showering or contact with water.
- [PV - Clinical] <font color="red">Erythromelalgia</font> is a unique PV symptom featuring red, warm, and painful extremities due to microvascular thrombosis.
- [PV - Clinical] <font color="red">Budd-Chiari syndrome</font> (hepatic vein thrombosis) should prompt suspicion of PV, especially in young women.
- [PV - Clinical] <font color="red">Hyperviscosity</font> in PV causes neurologic symptoms like vertigo, tinnitus, visual disturbances, and transient ischemic attacks (TIAs).
- [PV - Clinical] <font color="red">Acquired von Willebrand's disease</font> can occur in PV (and ET) due to high platelet counts leading to increased proteolysis of vWF multimers.
- [PV - Labs] <font color="red">Low to Normal Serum EPO</font> suggests PV as the cause of elevated hemoglobin, whereas High EPO suggests secondary polycythemia (e.g., hypoxia).
- [PV - Treatment] <font color="red">Phlebotomy</font> is used to reach target goals: Hemoglobin 14 g/dL and Hct <45% in males, and Hemoglobin 12 g/dL and Hct <42% in females.
- [PV - Treatment] <font color="red">Ruxolitinib</font> is a JAK1/2 inhibitor used for PV patients with splenomegaly or generalized pruritus.
- [PV - Treatment] <font color="red">Hydroxyurea</font> is a common chemotherapy for PV but has leukemogenic potential (can transform to leukemia).

[PRIMARY MYELOFIBROSIS (PMF)]
- [PMF - Definition] <font color="red">Primary Myelofibrosis</font> is the least common MPN, characterized by a "woody" or "tree bark-like" fibrotic bone marrow and massive splenomegaly.
- [PMF - Etiology] The <font color="red">mutation triad for PMF (*)</font> includes mutations in JAK2 (55%), CALR, or MPL.
- [PMF - Clinical] <font color="red">Extramedullary hematopoiesis (EMH)</font> in PMF causes massive splenomegaly and can lead to complications like portal hypertension or spinal cord compression.
- [PMF - Clinical] <font color="red">Splenic infarction</font> in PMF patients presents with fever, left upper quadrant (LUQ) pain, and pleuritic chest pain.
- [PMF - Diagnosis] <font color="red">Tear-drop shaped RBCs</font> on a peripheral smear are indicative of membrane damage from passage through a fibrotic marrow or the spleen.
- [PMF - Treatment] <font color="red">Allogenic bone marrow transplantation</font> is the only curative treatment for Primary Myelofibrosis.

[ESSENTIAL THROMBOCYTOSIS (ET)]
- [ET - Definition] <font color="red">Essential Thrombocytosis</font> is characterized by the overproduction of platelets without a definable cause, predominantly affecting females.
- [ET - Clinical] <font color="red">Spurious hyperkalemia</font> can occur in ET labs due to the release of potassium from high concentrations of platelets during clotting in the test tube.
- [ET - Diagnosis] <font color="red">Bone marrow biopsy in ET</font> shows megakaryocyte hypertrophy and hyperplasia.
- [ET - Complications] <font color="red">Tobacco use</font> is the most important risk factor for thrombosis in patients with Essential Thrombocytosis.
- [ET - Treatment] <font color="red">Aspirin</font> should NOT be given to ET patients with an acquired von Willebrand disease (platelet count >1,000,000) as it increases bleeding risk.
- [ET - Treatment] <font color="red">Anagrelide</font> is a non-cytotoxic platelet-lowering agent used in Essential Thrombocytosis.

[PLASMA CELL DISORDERS]
- [Plasma Cell Disorders] <font color="red">Plasma cells</font> are derived from B-lymphocytes and are responsible for producing antibodies (immunoglobulins).
- [Multiple Myeloma (MM) - Etiology] <font color="red">IL-6</font> is the key driver cytokine that promotes plasma cell proliferation in Multiple Myeloma.
- [MM - Clinical] The <font color="red">CRAB criteria</font> for Multiple Myeloma includes: Calcium (elevated), Renal failure, Anemia, and Bone lesions.
- [MM - Clinical] <font color="red">Bone pain</font> is the most common symptom of Multiple Myeloma, resulting from increased osteoclast activity and lytic lesions.
- [MM - Clinical] <font color="red">Bence Jones protein</font> refers to monoclonal light chains found in the urine of Multiple Myeloma patients.
- [MM - Labs] <font color="red">Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP)</font> shows a sharp "church spire" spike in the M component in monoclonal gammopathies.
- [MM - Labs] <font color="red">A decreased anion gap</font> in MM is caused by the presence of cationic M proteins.
- [MM - Prognosis] <font color="red">β2-microglobulin</font> is the best predictor of prognosis and a key component of the ISS staging for Multiple Myeloma.
- [MM - Treatment] <font color="red">Lenalidomide + Bortezomib + Dexamethasone (RVD)</font> is a standard first-line triplet regimen for MM.
- [MM - Treatment] <font color="red">Autologous stem cell transplant</font> is the standard of care for eligible MM patients but is not curative.
- [Waldenström’s Macroglobulinemia (WM)] <font color="red">IgM secretion</font> in WM causes hyperviscosity syndrome (headache, visual disturbance) and organomegaly, but unlike MM, there are no lytic bone lesions.
- [WM - Pathophysiology] The <font color="red">MYD88 L265P mutation</font> is found in >90% of Waldenström’s macroglobulinemia cases.
- [WM - Morphology] <font color="red">Rouleaux formation</font> (RBCs stacked like coins) is commonly seen on the peripheral smear in Waldenström’s.
- [POEMS Syndrome] <font color="red">POEMS syndrome</font> features sclerotic bone lesions, which differ from the lytic lesions seen in Multiple Myeloma.
- [Heavy Chain Disease] <font color="red">Gamma heavy chain disease (Franklin’s)</font> is classic for causing palatal edema which may compromise the airway.
- [Heavy Chain Disease] <font color="red">Alpha heavy chain disease (Seligmann’s)</font> is associated with Mediterranean lymphoma (IPSID) and Campylobacter jejuni, causing malabsorption.

[COMPARISON TIPS FOR EXAMS]
- [Differentiation: Bleeding History] In <font color="red">platelet disorders</font>, bleeding is mucosal (gums, epistaxis), while in <font color="red">clotting factor deficiencies</font> (like Hemophilia), bleeding is into cavities (joints, muscles).
- [Differentiation: Bone Marrow Failure] <font color="red">Aplastic Anemia</font> presents with a hypocellular (empty) marrow, whereas <font color="red">MDS</font> presents with a hypercellular or normal marrow with "sick-looking" (dysplastic) cells.
- [Differentiation: PCV vs Secondary Polycythemia] <font color="red">Polycythemia Vera</font> has LOW serum EPO levels, while <font color="red">Secondary Polycythemia</font> (from smoking or high altitude) has HIGH serum EPO levels.
- [Differentiation: Myeloproliferative Neoplasms] <font color="red">Polycythemia Vera</font> is dominated by RBC elevation; <font color="red">Essential Thrombocytosis</font> is dominated by Platelet elevation (>450,000); and <font color="red">Primary Myelofibrosis</font> is dominated by marrow fibrosis and massive splenomegaly.
- [Differentiation: Fibrosis vs Replacement] <font color="red">Myelofibrosis</font> is primary neoplastic fibrosis of the marrow, whereas <font color="red">Myelophthisis</font> is marrow replacement secondary to other causes like cancer or TB.
- [Differentiation: MM vs WM] <font color="red">Multiple Myeloma</font> is characterized by lytic bone lesions, renal failure, and hypercalcemia; <font color="red">Waldenström’s</font> is characterized by IgM, hyperviscosity, and organomegaly (no bone lesions).
- [Differentiation: Bone Lesions] <font color="red">Multiple Myeloma</font> shows lytic (dark/punched out) lesions, while <font color="red">POEMS syndrome</font> shows sclerotic (bright/dense) bone lesions.
- [Differentiation: PRCA] <font color="red">Pure Red Cell Aplasia</font> only affects the RBC line, whereas <font color="red">Aplastic Anemia</font> affects all cell lines (pancytopenia).
- [Differentiation: Reticulocytes] <font color="red">Reticulocyte count</font> is almost zero in Aplastic Anemia and PRCA (failure of production), whereas it may be high in states of peripheral destruction (hemolysis) or bleeding.
- [Differentiation: MDS vs Myeloproliferative] <font color="red">MDS</font> is a disorder of "dysfunction" (low counts, high risk AML), while <font color="red">Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (MPNs)</font> are disorders of "excess" (high counts).
- [Differentiation: Chromosomes] <font color="red">Monosomy 7</font> in MDS signals a poor prognosis, while the <font color="red">5q- syndrome</font> in MDS responds very well to Lenalidomide.
- [Differentiation: Mutations] <font color="red">JAK2 V617F</font> is present in >95% of PV cases, but only ~50-60% of ET and PMF cases.
- [Differentiation: Leukoplakia] <font color="red">Leukoplakia</font> in Dyskeratosis Congenita is a white patch that cannot be scraped off; if it can be scraped off, think Oral Candidiasis.
- [Differentiation: Splenomegaly] <font color="red">Splenomegaly</font> is a hallmark of Primary Myelofibrosis and is common in Waldenström’s, but it is highly UNLIKELY in Aplastic Anemia.
- [Differentiation: Etiology] <font color="red">Parvovirus B19</font> causes transient PRCA, while <font color="red">EBV</font> is more linked to total bone marrow failure (Aplastic Anemia).
- [Differentiation: Platelet Counts] In <font color="red">Essential Thrombocytosis</font>, bleeding can occur despite high platelet counts if the count is >1 million, because it causes an "acquired von Willebrand disease."
- [Differentiation: Treatment] <font color="red">Hydroxyurea</font> is used in PV and ET to lower counts, but it is avoided in PMF where ruxolitinib is preferred for splenomegaly.
- [Differentiation: Diagnosis] A <font color="red">DRY TAP</font> on bone marrow aspiration is classically associated with Myelofibrosis and Myelophthisis due to collagen/fibers.
- [Differentiation: PV Pruritus] <font color="red">Aquagenic pruritus</font> is itchiness after water contact, which is relatively unique to Polycythemia Vera among the blood cancers.
- [Differentiation: MM Staging] In <font color="red">Multiple Myeloma</font>, use β2-microglobulin and albumin for staging; do NOT rely only on the size of the M-spike for stage.

### I. ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS AND GENERAL FUNCTIONS

* For the <font color="red">GI Tract</font>, the system extends from the mouth to the anus, with organs compartmentalized by **sphincters** to regulate flow.
* The <font color="red">Mucosa</font> layer of the gut wall acts as the primary site for nutrient absorption and provides a barrier against luminal contents.
* The <font color="red">Smooth Muscle and Enteric Nerves</font> are responsible for controlling propulsion (motility) and secretion throughout the GI tract.
* The <font color="red">Serosa</font> provides the gut with a supportive foundation and integrates external inputs from the systemic environment.
* <font color="red">Lymphatic channels</font> in the GI tract assist in gut immune activity, while intrinsic nerves regulate fluid and local propulsion.
* In the <font color="red">Mouth</font>, food is mixed with **salivary amylase** (also found in the pancreas) before being delivered to the pharynx.
* The <font color="red">Lower Esophageal Sphincter (LES)</font> maintains a basal tone to prevent gastroesophageal reflux; stretching of this sphincter (e.g., in obese or postpartum patients) increases GERD risk.
* The <font color="red">Proximal Stomach</font> serves a storage function by relaxing to accommodate meals.
* The <font color="red">Distal Stomach</font> uses phasic contractions to grind food against the pylorus and mixes it with pepsin and acid (trituration).
* <font color="red">Gastric Acid</font> serves two high-yield functions: sterilizing the upper gut and enabling the secretion of **intrinsic factor** for Vitamin B12 absorption.
* The <font color="red">Small Intestine</font> is the site where most nutrient absorption occurs, aided by villi which increase surface area.
* The <font color="red">Duodenum</font> is the site where food mixes with bile (essential for lipid digestion) and pancreatic juice (bicarbonate and enzymes).
* The <font color="red">Ileum</font> is the specific site for the absorption of **bile acids** and **Vitamin B12**.
* The <font color="red">Ileocecal Valve</font> acts as a sphincter to prevent colo-ileal reflux, thereby limiting the density of microbes in the small intestine.
* The <font color="red">Colon</font> primarily dehydrates stool via to-and-fro contractions; luminal volume decreases from ~1500 mL in the ileum to ~200 mL in the rectum.
* The <font color="red">Liver</font> provides a protective function by detoxifying drugs and toxins absorbed via the portal circulation.

### II. CLASSIFICATION AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF GI DISEASES

| Topic/Category | Pathogenesis/Mechanism | Clinical Features/Examples |

* <font color="red">Small Bowel Obstruction</font> is most commonly caused by **adhesions** following surgery; early ambulation is advised as a preventive measure.
* <font color="red">Colonic Obstruction</font> is most commonly caused by **malignancy/cancer** in adults.
* <font color="red">Achalasia</font> is a motility disorder characterized by decreased peristalsis and incomplete relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES).

* <font color="red">Dysbiosis</font> refers to alterations in the gut microbiome and is linked to IBD, IBS flares, and celiac disease.
* <font color="red">Lynch Syndrome (HNPCC)</font> is an autosomal dominant condition characterized by a few polyps, primarily in the **proximal/right colon**, and an increased risk of gynecologic cancers.
* <font color="red">Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP)</font> is an autosomal dominant condition presenting with **hundreds to thousands of polyps**, primarily in the **left colon**.

### III. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS AND DIAGNOSTICS

* <font color="red">Visceral Pain</font> is described as vague, dull, and midline in location.
* <font color="red">Parietal Pain</font> is localized and well-defined, often associated with inflammation of the peritoneum.
* Under the <font color="red">9 Regions of the Abdomen</font>, RUQ pain (+ Murphy's sign) indicates cholecystitis, while RLQ pain at McBurney’s point suggests appendicitis.
* <font color="red">Heartburn</font> is a burning substernal sensation; patients are advised not to lie supine for at least 2 hours after eating to prevent reflux.
* <font color="red">Upper GI Bleeding</font> presents as **hematemesis** (vomiting blood) or **melena** (black, tarry stools); blood must stay in the stomach for ~14 hours to turn black.
* <font color="red">Lower GI Bleeding</font> typically presents as **bright red blood per rectum (hematochezia)**, often from hemorrhoids or diverticula.
* <font color="red">Mallory-Weiss Tears</font> are mucosal lacerations at the gastroesophageal junction caused by severe retching, often related to alcohol use.
* <font color="red">Dermatitis Herpetiformis</font> is a unique skin manifestation specifically associated with **Celiac disease**.
* <font color="red">Fecal Occult Blood Test (FOBT)</font> is the primary tool for assessing chronic, non-visible (occult) GI bleeding leading to iron deficiency anemia.
* <font color="red">Upper Endoscopy (EGD)</font> is the first-line diagnostic for ulcers, esophagitis, and Barrett’s esophagus, though it is normal in 85% of dyspepsia cases.
* <font color="red">Colonoscopy</font> remains the gold standard for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening, typically starting at age 45.
* <font color="red">Urea Breath Test</font> and Stool Antigen tests are used to detect active **H. pylori** infection.

### IV. ACUTE PANCREATITIS: ETIOLOGY AND PATHOGENESIS

* <font color="red">Acute Pancreatitis</font> is one of the most common inpatient GI diagnoses, with hospitalization rates increasing with age.
* <font color="red">Gallstones</font> are the leading cause of acute pancreatitis (30–60%); stones **<5 mm** are high-risk because they easily migrate and obstruct the ampulla of Vater.*****
* <font color="red">Alcohol</font> is the second most common cause of acute pancreatitis, accounting for 15–30% of cases.
* <font color="red">Hypertriglyceridemia</font> causes acute pancreatitis when serum TG levels exceed **1000 mg/dL**.*****
* <font color="red">DPP-4 Inhibitors</font> (incretin therapy) are associated with an increased risk of acute pancreatitis, whereas GLP-1 agonists (like Ozempic) show no clear increased risk in Harrison's.
* <font color="red">Autodigestion</font> is the accepted pathogenic theory where proteolytic enzymes are activated within the pancreatic acinar cells rather than the duodenum.*****
* <font color="red">Trypsin</font> is the central enzyme in pancreatitis; once activated, it triggers other enzymes like **elastase** and **phospholipase A2**.
* <font color="red">PSTI (SPINK1)</font> is a protective protease inhibitor that normally inactivates about 20% of intracellular trypsin activity to prevent autodigestion.
* <font color="red">PRSS1 (Cationic Trypsinogen Gene)</font> mutations are unique because they are sufficient to precipitate acute pancreatitis without any other risk factors.
* <font color="red">Low Intracellular Calcium</font> in the acinar cell cytosol is protective, as it promotes the destruction of spontaneously activated trypsin.
* In the <font color="red">Initial Phase</font> of pancreatitis, trypsin activation is mediated by the lysosomal hydrolase **cathepsin B**.
* The <font color="red">Third Phase</font> of pancreatitis involves systemic effects, including SIRS, ARDS, and multiorgan failure.

### V. ACUTE PANCREATITIS: ASSESSMENT AND DIAGNOSIS

* <font color="red">Acute Pancreatitis Diagnosis</font> requires 2 of 3 criteria: (1) typical epigastric pain radiating to the back, (2) Lipase/Amylase ≥3x ULN, (3) confirmatory imaging.
* <font color="red">Serum Lipase</font> is the preferred diagnostic test because it is **more specific** than amylase and remains elevated longer (7–14 days).
* <font color="red">Serum Amylase</font> can be **spuriously low** in cases of severe hypertriglyceridemia.
* <font color="red">Serum ALT >3x ULN</font> in the setting of pancreatitis is strongly associated with a **gallstone etiology**.
* <font color="red">Cullen’s Sign</font> is a faint blue discoloration around the umbilicus indicating hemoperitoneum.
* <font color="red">Grey Turner’s Sign</font> is a blue-red or green-brown discoloration of the flanks reflecting tissue breakdown of hemoglobin in severe necrotizing pancreatitis.
* <font color="red">Pleural Effusion</font> in acute pancreatitis occurs in 10-20% of cases and is most frequently **left-sided**.
* <font color="red">Hematocrit >44%</font> (hemoconcentration) and **BUN >22 mg/dL** (azotemia) on admission are strong predictors of more severe disease and higher mortality.
* <font color="red">Abdominal Ultrasound</font> is the recommended **initial** imaging modality to look for gallstones and ductal dilation.
* <font color="red">CT Scan with Contrast</font> should **NOT** be performed within the first 48 hours; it is best evaluated after **3–5 days** to assess for local complications like necrosis.*****

### VI. ACUTE PANCREATITIS: MANAGEMENT AND COMPLICATIONS

* <font color="red">Fluid Resuscitation</font> is the most important intervention; **Lactated Ringer's (LR)** is generally preferred over Normal Saline as it may decrease systemic inflammation.
* <font color="red">Mild Acute Pancreatitis</font> is self-limited, usually resolving in 3-7 days without organ failure; oral intake (low-fat solid diet) can be resumed quickly.
* <font color="red">Severe Acute Pancreatitis</font> is defined by **persistent organ failure (>48 hours)** involving the respiratory, cardiovascular, or renal systems.
* <font color="red">BISAP Score</font> (BUN >25, Impaired mental status, SIRS, Age >60, Pleural effusion) assesses in-hospital mortality risk within the first 24 hours.
* <font color="red">Enteral Nutrition</font> is preferred over TPN in severe pancreatitis because it maintains gut barrier integrity and limits bacterial translocation.*****
* <font color="red">Prophylactic Antibiotics</font> are **NO longer recommended** for severe acute pancreatitis as they do not improve survival and may promote fungal infections.
* <font color="red">Infected Pancreatic Necrosis</font> requires targeted antibiotics and definitive management via drainage or debridement.
* <font color="red">Pancreatic Pseudocysts</font> typically form **4–6 weeks** after the onset of pancreatitis; only symptomatic ones require drainage.
* <font color="red">Ascending Cholangitis</font> in the setting of gallstone pancreatitis mandates an **ERCP within 24–48 hours** of admission.
* <font color="red">Pancreatic Ascites</font> results from pancreatic duct disruption; the ascitic fluid will have a **high amylase level**.
* <font color="red">Splenic Vein Thrombosis</font> is a localized vascular complication of pancreatitis that can lead to gastric varices.

### VII. CHRONIC PANCREATITIS AND SECRETION REGULATION

* <font color="red">Secretin</font> is released by duodenal **S cells** in response to gastric acid and stimulates the secretion of water and bicarbonate from pancreatic ducts.
* <font color="red">Cholecystokinin (CCK)</font> is released by **I cells** in response to fats and amino acids, evoking an enzyme-rich secretion from acinar cells.
* <font color="red">Bicarbonate</font> in pancreatic juice is essential for neutralizing gastric acid and providing an optimal pH for digestive enzymes.
* <font color="red">Enterokinase</font> is an enzyme in the duodenal brush border that converts inactive trypsinogen into active trypsin.
* <font color="red">Negative Feedback</font> of pancreatic enzymes is mediated by trypsin; when proteases are free in the duodenum (late digestion), they blunt the CCK response.

### COMPARISON CHALLENGE: DIFFERENTIATING KEY GI CONCEPTS

1. <font color="red">Visceral vs. Parietal Pain</font>: **Visceral pain** is vague, dull, and midline (stretching of organs), while **Parietal pain** is sharp, well-localized, and associated with peritonitis/guarding.
2. <font color="red">Upper vs. Lower GI Bleed</font>: **Upper GI** sources (above Ligament of Treitz) cause melena or hematemesis; **Lower GI** sources cause hematochezia (bright red blood).
3. <font color="red">Cullen's vs. Grey Turner's Sign</font>: **Cullen's** is periumbilical (blue umbilicus); **Grey Turner's** is located on the flanks (sides). Both signify severe necrotizing pancreatitis.
4. <font color="red">Lynch Syndrome vs. FAP</font>: **Lynch** has few polyps, right-sided colon, and extraintestinal cancers; **FAP** has >100 polyps, left-sided colon, and 100% cancer risk if untreated.
5. <font color="red">Amylase vs. Lipase</font>: **Amylase** rises/falls quickly (short half-life) and is less specific; **Lipase** stays elevated longer (7-14 days) and is the preferred, more specific test.
6. <font color="red">Moderately Severe vs. Severe Pancreatitis</font>: **Mod-Severe** features transient organ failure that resolves in <48 hours; **Severe** features persistent organ failure lasting >48 hours.
7. <font color="red">Secretin vs. CCK</font>: **Secretin** stimulates bicarbonate and water (ductal cells); **CCK** stimulates digestive enzymes (acinar cells).
8. <font color="red">Interstitial vs. Necrotizing Pancreatitis</font>: **Interstitial** shows homogenous gland enhancement on CT; **Necrotizing** shows a lack of parenchymal enhancement (interrupted blood supply).
9. <font color="red">Type 1 vs. Type 3c Diabetes</font>: **Type 1** is autoimmune destruction of beta cells; **Type 3c** is pancreatogenic, caused by exocrine pancreatic disease (pancreatitis/cancer).
10. <font color="red">Maldigestion vs. Malabsorption</font>: **Maldigestion** is a failure of chemical breakdown (e.g., enzyme deficiency); **Malabsorption** is a failure of the mucosa to transport nutrients.
11. <font color="red">Gastric vs. Duodenal Ulcer Pain</font>: **Gastric ulcers** are often aggravated by meals; **Duodenal ulcers** are often relieved by meals.
12. <font color="red">Lactose Intolerance vs. Celiac Disease</font>: **Lactose intolerance** is a simple enzyme deficiency (no long-term damage); **Celiac** is an immune-mediated mucosal injury caused by gluten.
13. <font color="red">Secretory vs. Osmotic Diarrhea</font>: **Secretory diarrhea** persists during fasting; **Osmotic diarrhea** (malabsorption) improves when the patient stops eating.
14. <font color="red">IBD vs. IBS</font>: **IBD** (Crohn's/UC) is organic inflammation with visible damage/bleeding; **IBS** is a DGBI with no structural abnormalities.
15. <font color="red">PRSS1 vs. SPINK1</font>: **PRSS1** is a gain-of-function mutation in trypsinogen (autosomal dominant); **SPINK1** is a loss-of-function mutation in a trypsin inhibitor.
16. <font color="red">Murphy's vs. McBurney's Point</font>: **Murphy's sign** is RUQ pain on inspiration (Gallbladder); **McBurney's point** is RLQ tenderness (Appendix).
17. <font color="red">Psoas vs. Obturator Sign</font>: **Psoas** is pain on hip extension; **Obturator** is pain on internal rotation of the flexed hip. Both indicate appendicitis.
18. <font color="red">Enteral vs. Parenteral Nutrition</font>: **Enteral** (tube feeding) is preferred to maintain gut barrier; **Parenteral** (IV) is only for severe gut failure.
19. <font color="red">Prokinetics vs. Loperamide</font>: **Prokinetics** increase motility (for gastroparesis); **Loperamide** decreases motility (for diarrhea).
20. <font color="red">HNPCC vs. CRC Screening Age</font>: Normal CRC screening begins at **45**; Lynch/HNPCC families require much earlier screening due to genetic risk.
